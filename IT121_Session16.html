Overview

How to use this page

The final exam is cumulative: all 27 modules. Modules 1–13 were reviewed for the midterm in Session 8, so this page spends most of its words on Modules 14–27, which haven't been reviewed yet. The flashcards and practice exam include every session. Like the midterm review, nothing here is new. The point is to find your weak spots and go back to the right page.

  • Skim the module map. Mark any module where you can't explain the big ideas without notes
  • Read the commonly confused pairs, here and in Session 8
  • Learn the short lists and numbers in "Numbers & Lists." Exams love ordered steps
  • Run the flashcards filtered to Sessions 9–15 first, then all sessions
  • Take a 50-question practice exam, and reopen every session the report lists
Plan your time. If you have a week, spend the first half on Sessions 9–15, which haven't had a review yet. Spend the second half on mixed practice exams across all sessions. If you scored well on the midterm, a 25-question run filtered to each of Sessions 1–7 is enough of a refresher for that half.

Review 2

Commonly confused

Pairs from the second half that look alike on a quiz. The first-half pairs (IDS vs. IPS, DAC vs. MAC, TACACS+ vs. RADIUS, and more) are in Session 8. Review both.

These twoThe differenceWhere
Standard vs. extended ACLStandard filters on source address only and goes near the destination. Extended filters on source, destination, protocol, and ports, and goes near the sourceS9
Subnet mask vs. wildcard maskOpposites. In a wildcard mask, 0 means "must match" and 1 means "ignore." 0.0.0.255 matches a whole /24S9
Stateless vs. stateful firewallStateless checks each packet alone against rules. Stateful remembers connections, so replies to inside requests get back in automaticallyS9
Inspect vs. pass (ZPF)Inspect tracks the session and allows its replies. Pass forwards one way only, so replies need their own policyS10
Type 1 vs. Type 2 hypervisorType 1 runs on bare metal (data centers). Type 2 runs on top of an OS (VirtualBox on a laptop)S10
VM sprawl vs. VM escape vs. hyperjackingSprawl: more VMs than you can manage. Escape: a process breaks out of a VM to the host. Hyperjacking: an attacker takes over the hypervisor itselfS10
Symmetric vs. asymmetric encryptionSymmetric uses one shared key and is fast (AES). Asymmetric uses a key pair and is slow (RSA, DH), so it's used to exchange keys and signS11
Hash vs. HMAC vs. digital signatureA hash proves integrity only. An HMAC adds a shared secret key, proving origin too. A signature uses a private key, adding nonrepudiationS11
Encrypt with public vs. private keyReceiver's public key: confidentiality, only they can read it. Sender's private key: authentication, anyone can verify it came from the senderS11
Steganography vs. obfuscation vs. data maskingSteganography hides that a message exists. Obfuscation makes it confusing. Masking swaps real values for realistic fakesS11
Session data vs. full packet captureSession data (and NetFlow) records who talked to whom, when, and how much. A full packet capture also keeps the contentS12
Information vs. Success Audit eventInformation: an application, driver, or service worked. Success Audit: an audited security access attempt succeeded, such as a loginS12
False positive vs. false negativeFalse positive: an alert with no attack (wastes time). False negative: an attack with no alert (dangerous)S13
Deterministic vs. probabilistic analysisDeterministic assumes every exploit step succeeds (worst case, known facts). Probabilistic estimates each step's likelihood (real time, unknowns)S13
Data owner vs. data custodianThe owner decides classification and who gets access. The custodian implements the owner's decisionsS13
Passive vs. active reconnaissancePassive gathers information without touching the target (social media, public sites). Active interacts with it (scans)S15
Vulnerability assessment vs. pen test vs. risk analysisAssessment scans for known flaws. A pen test exploits them in a simulated attack. Risk analysis weighs likelihood, threat actors, and impact for this organizationS15
Qualitative vs. quantitative riskQualitative uses ratings and scenarios. Quantitative uses money values: SLE, ARO, ALES15
Corrective vs. recovery controlBoth act after a violation. Corrective fixes the problem and restores normal state. Recovery restores resources, such as backupsS15
Cyber Kill Chain vs. Diamond ModelThe Kill Chain is the seven steps of an attack over time. The Diamond Model describes one event by adversary, capability, infrastructure, and victimS15
DRP vs. BCPA DRP restores IT after a disaster. A BCP keeps the whole business running, including relocation and succession, and contains the DRPS15
Incremental vs. differential backupIncremental: changes since the last backup of any kind. Differential: changes since the last full backupS15

Applied

Worked examples

Final exam questions often describe one situation and ask about several modules at once. Each of these follows one incident across the course.

Sessions 1, 11, 12, 13, 15

The whole incident, start to finish

Bastion's Sguil queue shows a Snort alert: a Kiriyama General Hospital workstation is making thousands of DNS lookups with long random subdomains to one unfamiliar domain. Walk it from alert to closure: classify the alert, name the technique and Kill Chain step, say which data proves whether patient data left, and name the NIST incident response phases you'd move through.

Show answer

Classify: the packet capture confirms the encoded queries, so it's a true positive. Escalate it (Session 13).

Technique: DNS tunneling, used for command and control or exfiltration, which is Kill Chain step 6 or 7 (Session 12, Session 15).

Did data leave: NetFlow and DNS logs show volume and destination. A full packet capture shows the encoded content (Session 12). Patient data means a confidentiality breach with HIPAA consequences (Session 13).

Phases: detection and analysis → containment (isolate the PC, block the domain), eradication and recovery (rebuild, patch) → post-incident activity, including lessons learned (Session 15). Collect RAM first if the malware may be memory-resident (order of volatility).

Sessions 9, 10

ACL or zone-based firewall?

Tsukimi Sweets' router has four interfaces: two staff LANs, a guest Wi-Fi network, and the internet. Staff and guests should both reach the internet, guests must never reach staff, and replies must come back. Would you use ACLs or a ZPF, and what's the design?

Show answer

With four interfaces, a ZPF is easier to manage than per-interface ACLs (Session 10). Zones: STAFF (both staff LANs, so they talk freely), GUEST, and INTERNET.

Zone pairs: STAFF → INTERNET and GUEST → INTERNET, each with an inspect policy so replies return automatically. No GUEST → STAFF pair, so that traffic is dropped by default. The same could be done with extended ACLs placed close to the source (Session 9), but you'd need more of them, and the established keyword only approximates stateful inspection.

Sessions 10, 11, 13, 15

Moving the shop to the cloud

Tsukimi Sweets moves its online shop to IaaS and keeps taking credit cards. Which law or standard applies, what does Tsukimi still own under shared responsibility, which cryptography protects card data in transit and at rest, and how would you score a newly published flaw in the web server software?

Show answer

Standard: PCI DSS (Session 13).

Tsukimi owns: with IaaS, the OS, the application, the data, and access. The provider owns hardware and virtualization (Session 10).

Crypto: TLS (certificates from a trusted CA, then AES for the session) in transit. AES encryption at rest (Session 11).

Scoring: look up its CVE entry and CVSS score. A network attack vector with no privileges or user interaction usually scores High or Critical, so patch it first (Session 15).

Sessions 13, 15

Is the control worth it?

Kaidō Freight's leadership wants to know whether to buy a $6,000-a-year endpoint detection subscription. Bastion estimates that a ransomware incident would cost 30% of a $100,000 asset base, and expects one every two years without the control. Who should decide, what does the math say, and what type of control is it?

Show answer

Who: governance. Leadership, advised by security, decides risk responses. Bastion analysts advise but don't decide (Session 13).

Math: SLE = $100,000 × 0.30 = $30,000. ARO = 0.5. ALE = $15,000 per year. A $6,000 control that prevents most of that is justified. That's risk mitigation (Session 15).

Type: mainly detective and corrective: it detects threats and can quarantine or roll back (Session 15).

Sessions 7, 11, 13, 15

The former contractor

A former Kaidō Freight contractor logs in with an account that was never disabled and downloads shipping records. Which law did they break, which governance practice failed, how do investigators prove the evidence wasn't altered, and which Diamond Model features can you fill in?

Show answer

Law: the CFAA, accessing a computer without authorization (Session 13).

Failed practice: account management and offboarding. Access should end with the contract, under least privilege and need to know (Session 7).

Evidence: hash logs and images at collection, work on copies, and keep a chain of custody (Session 11, Session 15).

Diamond: adversary, the former contractor. Capability, valid stolen credentials. Infrastructure, the remote access path and source IP. Victim, Kaidō's file server (Session 15).
