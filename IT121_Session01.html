Overview

What Session 1 covers

Before firewalls or forensics, you need a shared vocabulary. This session sets the frame: what security protects, who attacks it, and the two big categories of attack: malicious code and manipulated people.

  • Explain the CIA triad and give a real example of each leg breaking
  • Distinguish threat, vulnerability, exploit, and risk — and how they chain together
  • Categorize threat actors by motivation and sophistication
  • Identify malware types by how they behave, not just their name
  • Recognize social engineering techniques and the psychology they exploit

Concept 1

The CIA Triad

Security isn't one goal. It's a balance of three, and most attacks can be described as breaking one of them.

LegWhat it protectsHow it breaksExample
ConfidentialityOnly authorized people/systems can view dataData breach, eavesdropping, weak access controlKiriyama General Hospital leaks patient records: a patient-privacy violation (HIPAA in the US)
IntegrityData is accurate and unaltered except by authorized actionTampering, man-in-the-middle modification, corruptionA Kiriyama General patient's allergy record is silently changed, risking a wrong dose
AvailabilitySystems and data are accessible when neededDoS/DDoS, ransomware, hardware failureRansomware locks Kiriyama General's entire EHR during an emergency
One data set, three kinds of harm: Kiriyama General's patient records can be stolen (confidentiality), altered (integrity), or locked away (availability). That's why the triad matters.
Think of it like… the lord's letters A castle sends orders by sealed letter. Confidentiality means only the addressee opens it. Integrity means the lord's seal proves nobody changed a word on the way. Availability means the courier actually gets through when the order is needed. A spy who reads the letter, a forger who edits it, and a bandit who stops the courier are three different failures, even though it's the same letter.

Concept 2

Threat · Vulnerability · Exploit · Risk

Four words that look similar but mean very different things. Learn them in order, because they chain together.

TermDefinitionCastle analogy
VulnerabilityA weakness that could be exploitedA gap in the stone wall, or a side gate left unbarred
ThreatAnything that could exploit a vulnerabilityA shinobi who could slip through it
ExploitThe specific mechanism/code used to take advantageThe grappling hook and climbing technique used on that gap
RiskLikelihood × impact of a threat successfully exploiting a vulnerabilityHow likely the castle is breached, and how bad it would be
If there were no vulnerabilities, there'd be no risk to manage. That's why so much of security is about shrinking the attack surface, every gate and wall section an attacker could try. You do it by patching software, enforcing strong passwords, and training employees.

Concept 3

Threat Actors

Who is behind the attacks? Threat actors differ by motivation and sophistication. Knowing which one you're up against changes what you defend first.

ActorMotivationSophisticationTypical behavior
Script kiddieBragging rights, curiosityLow — uses others' toolsCanned exploits, defacement
HacktivistPolitical / social messageLow–mediumWebsite defacement, data leaks
Organized crimeMoneyHigh, well-resourcedRansomware-as-a-service, card skimming
Nation-state / APTEspionage, sabotageVery high, well-fundedLong-dwell-time, stealthy access
InsiderMoney, revenge, or carelessnessVariesData theft, sabotage, or accidents
Insider threats are the hard ones. The person already has legitimate access. The attack isn't "getting in." It's someone with a valid account doing the wrong thing, on purpose or by accident. In castle terms, it's the retainer inside the walls who opens the gate.
Full dossiers on all five (MO, tools, countermeasures) are posted on the Bastion Security Threat Board.

Concept 4

Malware Types

Learn to identify malware by how it behaves, not just its name. The key question is usually: does it need a user, or does it spread on its own?

TypeHow it worksReal-world example
VirusAttaches to a host file; needs user action to spreadFile infectors
WormSelf-replicating, spreads across networks aloneConficker
Trojan horseDisguised as legit software; doesn't self-replicateFake "free" downloads
RansomwareEncrypts files/systems; demands paymentHospital EHR locks (availability!) — WannaCry (a self-spreading "cryptoworm" — worm delivery, ransomware payload) is the classic example
Spyware / adwareCovertly monitors, or floods with adsBrowser hijackers
GraywareUnwanted apps that behave in annoying or undesirable ways. Not always recognizable malware, but can still track location or push adsA "free" flashlight app that wants your location
RootkitHides deep in the OS for privileged, undetected accessBootkit infections
KeyloggerCaptures keystrokes to steal credentialsCredential theft
Logic bombTriggers on a condition/dateInsider sabotage
Virus vs. worm: a virus needs you to open the infected file. A worm spreads across the network with no user action at all. WannaCry is both at once: a worm for spreading and ransomware for its payload. That's why it doesn't sit cleanly in one row of this table.

Concept 5

Social Engineering

Attacks that exploit human psychology rather than technical flaws. No firewall can block a well-crafted lie.

Think of it like… the shinobi in disguise The strongest castle wall doesn't help if the guard opens the gate. Picture a shinobi dressed as a traveling merchant with a believable story and the right name to drop. Nothing gets climbed or broken: the guard lets him in. Every technique below is a version of that disguise. The defense is the same too: check the pass, not the costume.
TechniqueWhat it is
PhishingMass impersonation email/message to steal credentials or install malware. Spear-phishing targets a specific person; whaling targets executives. Vishing does it by phone; smishing does it by text.
PretextingFabricating a scenario/identity to extract info
BaitingLeaving infected media (USB drop) or an enticing offer
ScarewareCoaxes a victim into installing fake antivirus/security software by frightening them into believing they're already infected
Tailgating / piggybackingFollowing someone through a secured door
Shoulder surfingVisual theft of credentials/PINs
Dumpster divingRetrieving discarded information from the trash — not just paper, but electronic media left on old drives too

Hacker ethics: White, Gray, and Black Hat

This is a separate axis from motivation. It's about legality and consent: the same skill set, sorted by whether the hacker had permission and what they did with what they found.

TypeWhat defines them
White hatBreaks into systems with authorization to find and report weaknesses — a penetration tester or bug-bounty researcher
Gray hatFinds a vulnerability without authorization but discloses it rather than exploiting it — technically illegal, not malicious
Black hatExploits vulnerabilities without authorization for personal gain or harm — this is the "attacker" in every other table on this page

The psychology levers social engineers use

  • Urgency — "act within 2 hours or you're locked out"
  • Authority — "I'm from IT / the IRS / your boss"
  • Fear — a threat of consequence
  • Curiosity — "open this attachment"
  • Trust — impersonating someone the victim knows

Concept 6

Cloud Threats & Threat Intelligence

Two more pieces of Module 1: the cloud services organizations rent, which widen the attack surface, and the sources analysts use to keep up with new threats.

Threats to the public cloud

The public cloud is computing services hosted by a provider and shared across many organizations. Organizations subscribe to one of three models:

ModelWhat the provider gives youExample
Software as a Service (SaaS)Software hosted centrally in the cloud, used through a web browser or appWebmail or an online office suite
Platform as a Service (PaaS)The provider's hardware and tools, for developing, running, and managing your own applicationsA hosted platform for a custom web app
Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS)Virtual computing resources: servers, storage, and other infrastructure you rent access toVirtual servers in a provider's data center

Threat intelligence sources

SourceWhat it is
Common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVE)A public dictionary of known vulnerabilities, each with its own ID, sponsored by US-CERT and the US Department of Homeland Security
The dark webEncrypted web content that needs special software, authorization, or configuration to reach, and isn't indexed by normal search engines. Analysts watch it for stolen data and attack plans
Indicator of compromise (IOC)Evidence that a breach happened, with details about it, such as a malware signature or a malicious domain name
Automated Indicator Sharing (AIS)A CISA service for trading threat indicators in real time, using the structured language STIX and the exchange method TAXII
IOCs, and the indicators of attack (IOAs) that go with them, come back in Session 2.

Social engineering

The "IT" email

A Kaidō Freight dispatcher gets an email that looks like it's from the IT helpdesk: "Your password expires in 2 hours — click here to reset it before you're locked out." The link goes to a page that looks like Kaidō Freight's login.

Show answer

Technique: Phishing (specifically a credential-harvesting phish) using pretexting — it invents a false, urgent scenario ("locked out in 2 hours").

Psychological lever: urgency + authority (appears to come from IT).

Vulnerability exploited: not a software bug — the human tendency to act fast under pressure without verifying the sender.

Malware

Spot the malware type

Kiriyama General Hospital's imaging system suddenly displays a message: all patient scan files are encrypted, and a payment is demanded to restore access.

Show answer

Type: Ransomware.

CIA leg impacted: Availability — the data may be intact, but it's inaccessible.

Why it's dangerous here specifically: hospitals often pay quickly because availability failures can cost lives, which is exactly why attackers target them.

Threat actors

Who's behind it?

Tsukimi Sweets, a small family confectionery shop, has its website defaced overnight with a political message. The host restores it within hours. No data was stolen.

Show answer

Likely actor: Hacktivist — motivated by a message, not money or espionage, and low-impact/high-visibility fits the profile.

Contrast: if the same shop's POS system was quietly skimming card numbers for months, that pattern points to organized crime instead.

Threat · risk

Unlock the chain

Kaidō Freight runs an old, unpatched web server (a known weakness). An attacker downloads a ready-made tool for it and breaks in, forcing the company offline for a day while it recovers.

Show answer

Vulnerability: the unpatched server.

Exploit: the ready-made tool.

Threat: the attacker using that tool.

Risk: the chance of this happening × the damage of a day offline.

CIA triad

Which leg broke?

A user's browser is redirected to a fake banking page for 20 minutes, and a few login credentials are captured on a shared Wi-Fi. Nothing was changed and nothing is permanently unavailable.

Show answer

Primary leg: Confidentiality — credentials were viewed by an unauthorized party.

Why not the others: no data was altered (integrity) and the system kept working (availability).

Bonus: this scenario (redirect to a fake page) is a classic man-in-the-middle/social-engineering combo.
