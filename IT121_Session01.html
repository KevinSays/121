Overview

What Session 1 covers

Before we can talk about firewalls or forensics, we need a shared vocabulary. This session sets the frame: what security is actually protecting, who's attacking it, and the two big categories of attack — malicious code and manipulated people.

  • Explain the CIA triad and give a real example of each leg breaking
  • Distinguish threat, vulnerability, exploit, and risk — and how they chain together
  • Categorize threat actors by motivation and sophistication
  • Identify malware types by how they behave, not just their name
  • Recognize social engineering techniques and the psychology they exploit

Concept 1

The CIA Triad

Security isn't one goal — it's a three-way balance. Every attack can usually be described as breaking one of these three legs.

LegWhat it protectsHow it breaksExample
ConfidentialityOnly authorized people/systems can view dataData breach, eavesdropping, weak access controlA hospital leaks patient records — a HIPAA violation
IntegrityData is accurate and unaltered except by authorized actionTampering, man-in-the-middle modification, corruptionA patient's allergy record is silently changed — wrong dosage risk
AvailabilitySystems and data are accessible when neededDoS/DDoS, ransomware, hardware failureRansomware locks the entire EHR during an emergency
Hospital example, one data set: patient records can be stolen (confidentiality), altered (integrity), or locked away (availability). Same data, three different kinds of harm — that's why the triad matters.

Concept 2

Threat · Vulnerability · Exploit · Risk

Four words that look similar but mean very different things. Learn them in order — they chain together.

TermDefinitionHouse analogy
VulnerabilityA weakness that could be exploitedAn unlocked door
ThreatAnything that could exploit a vulnerabilityA burglar
ExploitThe specific mechanism/code used to take advantageLock-picking
RiskLikelihood × impact of a threat successfully exploiting a vulnerabilityThe chance your house actually gets robbed
If there were no vulnerabilities, there'd be no risk to manage. That's why so much of security is about shrinking the attack surface — patching software, enforcing strong passwords, training employees.

Concept 3

Threat Actors

Who's actually behind the attacks? They differ by motivation and sophistication — and knowing which you're up against changes what you defend first.

ActorMotivationSophisticationTypical behavior
Script kiddieBragging rights, curiosityLow — uses others' toolsCanned exploits, defacement
HacktivistPolitical / social messageLow–mediumWebsite defacement, data leaks
Organized crimeMoneyHigh, well-resourcedRansomware-as-a-service, card skimming
Nation-state / APTEspionage, sabotageVery high, well-fundedLong-dwell-time, stealthy access
InsiderMoney, revenge, or carelessnessVariesData theft, sabotage, or accidents
Insider threats are the hard ones. The person already has legitimate access — so the attack isn't "getting in," it's a person with a valid account doing the wrong thing, whether malicious or just careless.
Full dossiers on all five — MO, tools, countermeasures — are filed on the Bastion Security Threat Board.

Concept 4

Malware Types

Learn to identify malware by how it behaves, not just its name. The key question is usually: does it need a user, or does it spread on its own?

TypeHow it worksReal-world example
VirusAttaches to a host file; needs user action to spreadFile infectors
WormSelf-replicating, spreads across networks aloneConficker
Trojan horseDisguised as legit software; doesn't self-replicateFake "free" downloads
RansomwareEncrypts files/systems; demands paymentHospital EHR locks (availability!) — WannaCry (a self-spreading "cryptoworm" — worm delivery, ransomware payload) is the classic example
Spyware / adwareCovertly monitors, or floods with adsBrowser hijackers
RootkitHides deep in the OS for privileged, undetected accessBootkit infections
KeyloggerCaptures keystrokes to steal credentialsCredential theft
Logic bombTriggers on a condition/dateInsider sabotage
Virus vs. worm: a virus needs you to open the infected file; a worm spreads across the network with no user action at all. WannaCry is both at once — a worm for propagation, ransomware for payload — which is why it doesn't sit cleanly in just one row of this table.

Concept 5

Social Engineering

Attacks that exploit human psychology rather than technical flaws. No firewall can block a well-crafted lie.

TechniqueWhat it is
PhishingMass impersonation email/message to steal credentials or install malware. Spear-phishing targets a specific person; whaling targets executives. Vishing does it by phone; smishing does it by text.
PretextingFabricating a scenario/identity to extract info
BaitingLeaving infected media (USB drop) or an enticing offer
ScarewareCoaxes a victim into installing fake antivirus/security software by frightening them into believing they're already infected
Tailgating / piggybackingFollowing someone through a secured door
Shoulder surfingVisual theft of credentials/PINs
Dumpster divingRetrieving discarded information from the trash — not just paper, but electronic media left on old drives too

Hacker ethics: White, Gray, and Black Hat

A separate axis from motivation — this one's about legality and consent. The same skill set, sorted by whether the hacker had permission and what they did with what they found.

TypeWhat defines them
White hatBreaks into systems with authorization to find and report weaknesses — a penetration tester or bug-bounty researcher
Gray hatFinds a vulnerability without authorization but discloses it rather than exploiting it — technically illegal, not malicious
Black hatExploits vulnerabilities without authorization for personal gain or harm — this is the "attacker" in every other table on this page

The psychology levers social engineers use

  • Urgency — "act within 2 hours or you're locked out"
  • Authority — "I'm from IT / the IRS / your boss"
  • Fear — a threat of consequence
  • Curiosity — "open this attachment"
  • Trust — impersonating someone the victim knows

Social engineering

The "IT" email

An employee gets an email that looks like it's from the IT helpdesk: "Your password expires in 2 hours — click here to reset it before you're locked out." The link goes to a page that looks like the company login.

Show answer

Technique: Phishing (specifically a credential-harvesting phish) using pretexting — it invents a false, urgent scenario ("locked out in 2 hours").

Psychological lever: urgency + authority (appears to come from IT).

Vulnerability exploited: not a software bug — the human tendency to act fast under pressure without verifying the sender.

Malware

Spot the malware type

A hospital's imaging system suddenly displays a message: all patient scan files are encrypted, and a payment is demanded to restore access.

Show answer

Type: Ransomware.

CIA leg impacted: Availability — the data may be intact, but it's inaccessible.

Why it's dangerous here specifically: hospitals often pay quickly because availability failures can cost lives, which is exactly why attackers target them.

Threat actors

Who's behind it?

A small local bakery's website gets defaced overnight with a political message, then restored by the host within hours. No data was stolen.

Show answer

Likely actor: Hacktivist — motivated by a message, not money or espionage, and low-impact/high-visibility fits the profile.

Contrast: if the same bakery's POS system was quietly skimming card numbers for months, that pattern points to organized crime instead.

Threat · risk

Unlock the chain

A company runs an old, unpatched web server (a known weakness). An attacker finds and downloads a ready-made tool for it and breaks in, forcing the firm offline for a day as it recovers.

Show answer

Vulnerability: the unpatched server.

Exploit: the ready-made tool.

Threat: the attacker using that tool.

Risk: the chance of this happening × the damage of a day offline.

CIA triad

Which leg broke?

A user's browser is redirected to a fake banking page for 20 minutes, and a few login credentials are captured on a shared Wi-Fi. Nothing was changed and nothing is permanently unavailable.

Show answer

Primary leg: Confidentiality — credentials were viewed by an unauthorized party.

Why not the others: no data was altered (integrity) and the system kept working (availability).

Bonus: this scenario (redirect to a fake page) is a classic man-in-the-middle/social-engineering combo.
