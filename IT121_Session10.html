Concept 1 · Module 16.1

ZPF Overview

Instead of attaching rules to each interface, a ZPF groups interfaces into zones and writes rules between zones.

Two ways to build an IOS firewall

Cisco IOS routers support two firewall configuration models:

Classic FirewallThe older model. Inspection rules and ACLs are applied interface by interface. It gets hard to manage once a router has more than two or three interfaces, because every interface needs its own ACLs and inspection actions.
Zone-based policy firewall (ZPF)Interfaces are assigned to zones, and inspection policies are applied to traffic moving between zones. Adding an interface usually just means putting it in the right zone.

Both models can be enabled on the same router at the same time, but they can't be combined on a single interface. An interface that's a zone member can't also use classic IP inspection.

Benefits of a ZPF

  • It's not dependent on ACLs.
  • The router's security posture is to block unless explicitly allowed.
  • Policies are easy to read and troubleshoot, because they're written in the Cisco Common Classification Policy Language (C3PL), the same structured class-map / policy-map language IOS uses elsewhere.
  • One policy affects any given traffic, instead of needing multiple ACLs and inspection actions.
  • Virtual and physical interfaces can both be grouped into zones, and policies apply to unidirectional traffic between zones.

Designing the zones

Designing a ZPF comes before typing any commands. The steps:

1 · Determine the zonesDivide the infrastructure into zones with different security levels. Focus on separating the network into zones, not on how each zone will be enforced yet.
2 · Establish policies between zonesFor each pair of source and destination zones, define the sessions that clients in the source zone may open to servers in the destination zone.
3 · Design the physical infrastructureWith the zones and traffic requirements known, plan the physical layout, keeping security and availability in mind. This includes how many devices sit between the most-secure and least-secure zones.
4 · Identify subsets and merge requirementsFor each firewall device, identify zone subsets connected to its interfaces, and merge the traffic requirements into a policy for that device.

Most designs end up with a few common zones. The private (inside, trusted) zone holds internal LANs. The public (outside, untrusted) zone holds interfaces that connect outside the organization. A DMZ zone holds public-facing servers when there is one. And every ZPF router has a built-in self zone, covered next.

A gate with its own rulebook works fine when a fortress has two gates. Picture one with a dozen, each keeping its own separate list. Every change means updating every list, and sooner or later two of them disagree. A ZPF fixes that by moving the rules off the gates.

Think of it like… the wards of the castle town Session 9's gate guard read one list at one gate. A ZPF organizes the whole town into wards instead: the samurai quarter, the merchant quarter, the road outside the walls. People move freely inside their own ward. Crossing from one ward into another needs a written order from the magistrate that names both wards and the direction: "merchants may go out to the road." That order doesn't let road travelers walk in uninvited. A ward with no order covering a crossing stays shut to it. The castle keep itself is its own ward, the self zone, with its own rules.
Zones and the "blocked unless a policy allows" default were introduced in Session 9. Stateful firewalls, which the ZPF inspect action builds on, are in Session 9's firewall section.

Concept 2 · Module 16.2

ZPF Operation

Three actions, plus a short table of rules that says what happens to every packet. Learn the table and most ZPF questions answer themselves.

The three actions

Each policy matches traffic into classes and gives each class one action:

InspectStateful inspection. The router tracks the connection and automatically allows the return traffic from the same flow. Similar to the classic ip inspect command. The usual choice for traffic leaving a trusted zone.
DropDiscards the traffic, like a deny statement in an ACL. A log option can record what was dropped.
PassForwards the traffic without tracking the connection, like a permit statement in an ACL. Because pass is stateless and one-way, the return traffic needs its own policy in the opposite direction.

Zone pairs are one-way

A policy is applied to a zone pair: a source zone and a destination zone. The pair has a direction. A PRIVATE → PUBLIC zone pair covers sessions that start in PRIVATE. It says nothing about sessions that start in PUBLIC.

With inspect, that one-way pair is enough for normal browsing: the router remembers the outbound session and lets the replies back in. To let outside hosts start sessions to a DMZ web server, you'd need a separate PUBLIC → DMZ zone pair with its own policy.

Rules for transit traffic

Transit traffic passes through the router from one interface to another. What happens to it depends on whether each interface is in a zone and whether a policy exists:

Read the table as three ideas. Traffic between two interfaces that are both outside every zone isn't touched, which is how the router behaved before the ZPF existed. Traffic between a zone member and a non-member is always dropped. And traffic between two different zones crosses only when a zone pair and a policy say so.

Rules for the self zone

The self zone is the router itself. It's system-defined, it has no interfaces assigned to it, and it covers traffic sent to the router (an SSH session to manage it, a routing update) or from the router. The self zone is the exception to the default deny:

So until you write a self-zone policy, anyone in any zone can reach the router itself. If you want to limit who can manage the router, you configure zone pairs with self as the source or destination.

Concept 3 · Module 16.3

Configure a ZPF

Five steps, always in this order. Each step builds a piece that the next step refers to by name.

Step 1: Create the zones

R1(config)# zone security PRIVATE
R1(config-sec-zone)# exit
R1(config)# zone security PUBLIC
R1(config-sec-zone)# exit

The self zone already exists, so you never create it.

Step 2: Identify traffic with a class map

A class map sorts traffic into a named class. match-any means a packet only has to meet one of the match conditions. match-all means it has to meet every one.

R1(config)# class-map type inspect match-any HTTP-TRAFFIC
R1(config-cmap)# match protocol http
R1(config-cmap)# match protocol https
R1(config-cmap)# match protocol dns
R1(config-cmap)# exit

Besides match protocol, a class map can use match access-group to match an ACL, or match class-map to nest another class map.

Step 3: Define the action with a policy map

A policy map lists classes and gives each one an action: inspect, drop, or pass.

R1(config)# policy-map type inspect PRIV-TO-PUB
R1(config-pmap)# class type inspect HTTP-TRAFFIC
R1(config-pmap-c)# inspect
R1(config-pmap-c)# exit
R1(config-pmap)# exit

Every inspect policy map also ends with a built-in class class-default, which catches anything that matched no other class. Its default action is drop, so traffic you didn't name is discarded, the same idea as an ACL's implicit deny.

Step 4: Apply the policy to a zone pair

R1(config)# zone-pair security PRIV-PUB source PRIVATE destination PUBLIC
R1(config-sec-zone-pair)# service-policy type inspect PRIV-TO-PUB
R1(config-sec-zone-pair)# exit

The policy is applied to the zone pair, not to a zone and not to an interface. Use self as the source or destination for a policy that covers traffic to or from the router.

Step 5: Assign interfaces to zones

R1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/0/1
R1(config-if)# zone-member security PRIVATE
R1(config-if)# interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0
R1(config-if)# zone-member security PUBLIC

This step goes last on purpose. The moment an interface joins a zone, the transit rules apply to it. If you did this first, traffic would start dropping before the policies that allow it existed.

Verify

CommandShows
show run | begin class-mapThe ZPF section of the running configuration

Configuration considerations

Things to remember
  • An interface can belong to only one security zone.
  • Traffic between interfaces in the same zone isn't filtered by any policy.
  • Traffic between a zone member and an interface that isn't in any zone is dropped.
  • An interface can't be a zone member and use classic IP inspection at the same time.
  • Traffic to and from the router itself (the self zone) passes until you write a self-zone policy.
Common mistakes
  • Using pass for outbound web traffic, then wondering why replies never arrive.
  • Adding a new interface and forgetting zone-member security. It can't talk to any zoned interface.
  • Writing the zone pair in the wrong direction.
  • Assigning interfaces to zones before the policies exist, and cutting off traffic (including your own remote session).

Concept 4 · Module 17.1

Virtualization and Cloud Computing

Cloud computing runs on virtualization. Start with how one physical server becomes many, then how those servers get rented out.

Virtualization

Virtualization separates the operating system from the hardware. A hypervisor is the software, firmware, or hardware that creates and runs virtual machines (VMs), letting several independent operating systems share one physical computer. Each VM thinks it has its own CPU, memory, disk, and network card.

Type 1 hypervisor ("bare metal")Installed directly on the hardware, with no host OS underneath. Used in data centers and clouds because it's efficient. Examples: VMware ESXi, Microsoft Hyper-V, Xen, KVM.
Type 2 hypervisor ("hosted")Runs as an application on top of an existing OS. Used on desktops for testing and labs. Examples: Oracle VirtualBox, VMware Workstation. The course's lab VM runs on one of these.

Cloud computing

Cloud computing delivers computing resources (servers, storage, applications) on demand over a network, usually paid for by use. The provider owns the data centers. NIST defines three service models and four deployment models:

Service modelWhat you rentExample
Software as a Service (SaaS)A finished application. The provider runs everything underneath it.Web email, an online office suite, a hosted patient-records app
Platform as a Service (PaaS)A platform to build and run your own apps on, without managing the servers or OS.A managed web-app hosting platform or managed database
Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS)Virtual machines, storage, and networks. You install and manage the OS and everything above it.Renting VMs to run your own web servers
Deployment modelWho it's for
Public cloudServices offered to the general public over the internet by a provider
Private cloudUsed by one organization only. Can be run in-house or by a third party
Hybrid cloudTwo or more clouds (for example, part private and part public) connected so data and apps can move between them
Community cloudShared by organizations with common needs, such as several healthcare providers with the same compliance rules

Shared responsibility

In the cloud, security is split between the provider and the customer. The provider always secures the physical data center and the hardware. How much more it covers depends on the service model. The further up the stack you rent, the less you manage, but you always own your data and who has access to it.

Many cloud breaches happen in the customer's share: a storage bucket left public, an admin account without multifactor authentication, or a VM nobody patched.

The split confuses people because the provider is clearly guarding something. It's easy to assume that means it's guarding everything. A picture from the castle town makes the line easier to see.

Think of it like… renting space in the merchant's storehouse A merchant house in the castle town rents out space in its kura, the thick-walled fireproof storehouses along the canal. The owner keeps the walls sound, the roof tiled, and a watchman at the outer door. That's the provider's share. But what goes inside your section, who you give a key to, and whether you lock your own chests is on you. If you hand a key to a stranger or leave your chest open, the watchman at the door won't save it. Rent more (a fully staffed shop instead of bare floor) and the house takes on more of the work. That's IaaS versus SaaS. It never takes over your keys.

Threats to the cloud

Cloud misconfigurationA cloud resource is set up incorrectly, leaving it open to attack. Example: a storage container shared publicly by mistake.
Compromised account credentialsUser accounts or access privileges aren't secured, and threat actors hijack them.
Inside threatAn employee, contractor, or business partner compromises the cloud service, on purpose or by accident.
Poor cloud security architecture strategyThe shared security responsibilities between customer and provider aren't understood or implemented correctly.
Limited cloud usage visibilityThe customer can't fully see how the cloud service is used, so it's harder to tell safe activity and files from malicious ones.
Data breachesProtected or sensitive data is accessed by someone not authorized to see it. Often the end result of the threats above.
Insider threats as a category of threat actor are in Session 1. Multifactor authentication and the principle of least privilege are in Session 7.

Concept 6 · Module 17.3

Cloud Infrastructure Security

The cloud still has networks, firewalls, and admin consoles. They're just virtual.

Mitigating threats to the cloud platform

MicrosegmentationAlso called hypersegregation. Uses virtual network topologies to run many smaller, isolated networks without buying extra hardware. Gives finer-grained control over traffic and workflows between workloads, so one compromised VM can't freely reach the rest.
Virtual security appliancesFirewalls and IPSs that run as VMs inside the cloud, filtering traffic between virtual networks the same way physical ones do between physical networks.
Site-to-site VPN for hybrid cloudsA hybrid cloud links a private cloud to a public one. A site-to-site VPN between them protects the confidentiality of the traffic crossing the internet.
Securing the management planeThe cloud console and its APIs can create, change, or delete everything. Protect admin accounts with multifactor authentication and least privilege, and log what they do.
Configuration managementUse reviewed, consistent configurations and check them regularly, because misconfiguration is one of the most common cloud threats.
Monitoring and visibilityCollect the provider's logs and watch them, to counter limited cloud usage visibility.

Microsegmentation is the ZPF idea from the first half of this session carried into the cloud: draw small zones and allow only the traffic that has a reason to cross.

Network segmentation and zones of risk on premises are in Session 7. VPNs as a security device are in Session 4.

Concept 7 · Module 17.4

Cloud Application Security

Security gets built into an application as it moves through development, not bolted on afterward.

With SaaS, the provider owns the application. With PaaS and IaaS, the customer builds and deploys its own applications, so application security is mostly the customer's job. Cloud apps typically move through separate environments on their way to users:

EnvironmentPurposeSecurity focus
DevelopmentDevelopers write and change the codeSecure coding practices, input validation, no secrets (passwords, keys) written into the code
TestingCheck that the code works and find bugsCode review and vulnerability scanning before anything moves on
StagingA copy that matches production as closely as possibleVerify the application runs correctly under the required security settings before release
ProductionThe live system real users depend onMonitoring, patching, and change control

The staging environment is the one most often tested: it's where you confirm the software works with production's real security settings, such as the same firewall rules, permissions, and encryption, so nothing surprises you after go-live. Keeping the environments separate also means a mistake in development can't touch live data.

Cloud security applications. Beyond building apps securely, organizations add security services delivered from the cloud, the CSA's "Security as a Service" domain: cloud-hosted email and web filtering, identity services with multifactor authentication, and vulnerability scanning. Session 9's firewall types also come in cloud-hosted form.

Concept 8 · Module 17.5

Cloud Data Security

Data is the one thing the customer always owns. Protect it in every state it's in.

States of data

Data at restStored on a disk, in a database, or in cloud storage. Protect with: encryption at rest, access controls, backups.
Data in transitMoving between devices, networks, or components. Protect with: encrypted protocols such as TLS (HTTPS) and VPNs.
Data in processBeing used or changed by an application. Also called data in use. Protect with: access controls, input validation, and keeping processing on trusted systems.

Encryption and integrity

Encryption turns readable plaintext into unreadable ciphertext. There are two classes of encryption algorithm. Symmetric algorithms use one pre-shared key to encrypt and decrypt. Asymmetric algorithms use a pair of keys, one to encrypt and the other to decrypt. Cryptography gets a full session of its own in Module 18.

Encryption protects confidentiality. For integrity, compare a checksum or hash of the original file with one of the copy you received. If the two match, the file wasn't changed in transit.

Governing the data

  • Classify data by sensitivity, so the most sensitive data gets the strongest controls.
  • Know where the data is stored. Laws differ between countries, and some data (patient records, for example) has strict rules about where it can be stored and who can see it.
  • Control and review who has access, and remove access when it's no longer needed.
  • Plan for deletion: know how data is removed when you leave a provider.
The CIA triad, and hashing for integrity, are in Session 6. Encryption in depth is Session 11 (Module 18).

Concept 9 · Module 17.6

Protecting VMs

Every VM is a full computer that needs the same care as a physical one, and the hypervisor under them needs more.

Threats to virtual machines

VM sprawlAn organization has more VMs than it can effectively control and manage. VMs are easy to spin up and easy to forget. Forgotten ones go unpatched and unmonitored.
VM escapeA process breaks out of a VM's isolation and interacts with the host or hypervisor directly, which can expose every other VM on that host.
HyperjackingAn attacker takes control of the hypervisor itself, and then can see or attack every VM it runs, and other data center devices.
Insecure VM imagesA template with an old, unpatched OS gets copied into every new VM built from it.
Invisible VM-to-VM trafficTraffic between VMs on the same host never crosses a physical wire, so physical firewalls and IDSs never see it.

Protecting them

  • Harden and patch the hypervisor, and tightly restrict who can manage it.
  • Patch the VMs and the images they're built from, not just the running machines.
  • Keep an inventory of VMs with owners, and delete the ones nobody needs, to stop sprawl.
  • Use microsegmentation and virtual firewalls so VM-to-VM traffic is filtered and visible.
  • Run endpoint protection inside each VM, the same as on a physical host.
The trade-off with virtual security appliances. A virtual firewall or IPS, or a security agent inside every VM, uses the same CPU and memory as the workloads it protects. It can become a traffic bottleneck or overload the processor. Evaluate the performance cost before adding one.
Endpoint protection (antimalware, host-based firewalls, patching) is in Session 5.

ZPF actions

The web pages that never load

A technician at Tsukimi Sweets set up a ZPF on the shop's router: a PRIVATE → PUBLIC zone pair with a policy that matches HTTP, HTTPS, and DNS and gives the class the pass action. Staff can't load any website. The firewall log shows outbound requests leaving. What's wrong, and what's the fix?

Show answer

Cause: pass is stateless and one-way. The requests go out, but nothing allows the replies back from PUBLIC to PRIVATE, and with no zone pair in that direction they're dropped.

Fix: change the action to inspect. The router then tracks each outbound session and lets its replies back in automatically. A second PUBLIC → PRIVATE pass policy would also work, but it would let in any matching traffic, not just replies, so inspect is the right choice.

Transit rules

The new interface

Kiriyama General Hospital's branch clinic router already runs a working ZPF with PRIVATE and PUBLIC zones. An admin connects a new lab network to GigabitEthernet0/0/2, gives it an IP address, and doesn't touch the firewall configuration. The lab can't reach the clinic LAN or the internet. Why?

Show answer

G0/0/2 isn't a member of any zone, and every other interface is. Under the transit rules, traffic between a zone member and a non-member is always dropped, whatever policies exist.

Fix: decide which zone the lab belongs in. If it should be treated like the clinic LAN, add zone-member security PRIVATE on G0/0/2. If it should be kept apart, create a LAB zone and write zone pairs and policies for the traffic it actually needs.

ZPF design

Opening the DMZ web server

Kaidō Freight's edge router has three zones: PRIVATE, PUBLIC, and DMZ, where its shipment-tracking web server sits. Customers on the internet must reach the server on HTTPS. Staff must reach it too. Nothing in the DMZ should ever start a connection into PRIVATE. Which zone pairs do you configure?

Show answer

PUBLIC → DMZ: a policy that inspects HTTPS only. Customers start the sessions, and inspect lets the server's replies back out.

PRIVATE → DMZ: a policy that inspects the traffic staff need (HTTPS, plus management protocols for admins).

DMZ → PRIVATE: configure nothing. With no zone pair, every new connection from the DMZ into PRIVATE is dropped by default. That's the safety net if the web server is ever compromised. A PRIVATE → PUBLIC inspect policy for staff internet access would round out the design.

Shared responsibility

Whose job is the patient portal?

Kiriyama General Hospital moves its patient portal to a SaaS product. A manager says, "Great, security is the vendor's problem now." Which parts still belong to the hospital?

Show answer

With SaaS the provider secures the facility, hardware, hypervisor, OS, and the application itself. The hospital still owns its data and access: who gets accounts, removing accounts when staff leave, requiring multifactor authentication, configuring sharing settings correctly, and checking that the vendor's data storage meets patient-privacy rules (the CSA's Compliance and Audit Management and Information Governance domains).

Believing otherwise is the "poor cloud security architecture strategy" threat: the shared responsibilities aren't understood or carried out.

Protecting VMs

Forty forgotten servers

During an audit of Kaidō Freight's IaaS account, Bastion finds 40 running VMs that nobody on the current IT team recognizes. Most were built for a project two years ago, from an image that hasn't been patched since. Name the problem and three actions to take.

Show answer

Problem: VM sprawl, more VMs than the organization can control and manage, made worse by an insecure image.

Actions: (1) inventory every VM and assign an owner; (2) shut down and delete the ones nobody needs, after checking for data that must be kept; (3) patch the image and rebuild or patch the VMs that stay; and put a rule in place that new VMs need an owner and an end date.

Cloud infrastructure

Linking the two clouds

Tsukimi Sweets runs its order database in a private cloud and its online shop front end in a public cloud. Order data crosses the internet between them. What protects the confidentiality of that traffic, and what would limit the damage if the public shop's web server were compromised?

Show answer

Confidentiality: a site-to-site VPN between the public and private clouds, the standard protection for a hybrid cloud link.

Containment: microsegmentation. Put the web server in its own small segment, with rules allowing it to reach only the database port it needs. A compromised web server then can't freely explore the rest of the environment.
