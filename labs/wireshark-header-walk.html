Wireshark Header Walk

Every dossier in the Bastion Security kit describes an attack from the outside — MO, tools, countermeasure. This lab is where you see the actual header fields those dossiers keep referencing, on traffic you captured yourself. A Tier-1 analyst who can't read a packet capture is guessing.

Companion to Session 2 · Module 3.1 Time ~30–40 min Difficulty Beginner
↩ Contents Objectives Part 1 Part 2 Part 3 Part 4 Part 5 Reflection

Objectives

What you'll do

  • Capture live network traffic with Wireshark
  • Locate a TCP three-way handshake inside a capture
  • Identify and interpret every field in a real IPv4 packet header
  • Compare what you find to the IPv6 header, live or by reference

Required resources

  • A computer with Wireshark installed (free — wireshark.org)
  • A working internet connection
  • About 30–40 minutes
Scope note: this lab only captures and reads your own traffic on your own machine. That's legal and normal — it's the same thing your browser's network tab does. Capturing traffic that isn't yours, on a network you don't have authorization to monitor, is a different thing entirely and is out of bounds for this exercise.

Part 1

Capture some traffic

Get Wireshark running and grab a small, ordinary capture — nothing exotic needed.

  1. Open Wireshark and look at the list of interfaces on the start screen. Pick the one showing live traffic (usually your Wi-Fi or Ethernet adapter — it'll have a moving activity graph next to it).
  2. Double-click that interface to start capturing. Traffic should immediately start scrolling in the packet list.
  3. Open a browser and visit any website. HTTP or HTTPS both work fine — this lab only cares about the IP and TCP headers, not the payload, so encryption doesn't get in the way.
  4. After the page loads, click the red square (Stop) button in Wireshark to end the capture.

Part 2

Find the three-way handshake

Somewhere in your capture is the exact SYN / SYN-ACK / ACK sequence from Session 2's TCP diagram. Find it.

  1. In the display filter bar at the top, type tcp.flags.syn==1 and press Enter. This shows only packets with the SYN flag set — your handshake's opening two packets.
  2. You should see a pair: one going from your machine outward (the SYN) and one coming back (the SYN-ACK, which also has the SYN flag set). Note the frame number of the outgoing SYN.
  3. Clear the filter and scroll to that frame number. The very next packet — from your machine, small, no data — is the final ACK. Those three frames are your handshake.
Why this matters: a SYN flood (Session 2) is this exact exchange, deliberately never finished. You just watched the version that completes normally — knowing what "normal" looks like is what makes the abnormal version recognizable in a real incident.

TTL tell: common default starting TTLs are 64 (Linux/macOS), 128 (Windows), and 255 (some network gear). Your captured TTL will be a little lower than the true default, since it's decremented at every router hop between you and the server — the gap tells you roughly how many hops away it is.

Now find the SYN-ACK. Its Acknowledgment Number should be exactly one more than the Sequence Number you just wrote down for the SYN — that's TCP confirming "I got your opening byte, send the next one." Session hijacking (Session 2) is exactly this number being predicted or stolen.

Reflection

Write it up

Short answers, in your own words. These aren't auto-graded — this is where you show you understand what you just looked at, not just that you copied values into a table.

1.Based on your captured TTL and the common OS default TTLs (64 / 128 / 255), what can you infer about the server you connected to, or about how many hops away it is?

2.Which single header field would an attacker have to forge to carry out IP spoofing? Why does forging just that one field work — what doesn't verify it?

3.Explain, in your own words, why the Acknowledgment Number on the SYN-ACK packet is exactly one more than the Sequence Number on the SYN.

4.If a router somewhere on the internet ignored the TTL/Hop Limit field entirely, what specific failure mode becomes possible?

5.You looked at one packet out of an entire handshake. What would change about your worksheet if you'd expanded the SYN-ACK packet instead of the SYN — which fields would differ, and which would stay the same?
