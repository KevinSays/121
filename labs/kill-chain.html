Kill Chain Reconstruction

Session 15 lists the seven steps of the Cyber Kill Chain in order. Real incidents don't arrive that way. They arrive as a pile of log lines from different tools, out of order, with some noise mixed in. In this lab you get the evidence pack from one Bastion case, sort it into the Kill Chain, rebuild what the attacker did, and decide where the chain should have been broken.

Companion to Session 15 · Module 27.2–27.3 Time ~45 min Difficulty Intermediate
↩ Contents Objectives The Case Part 1 Part 2 Part 3 Part 4 Reflection

Objectives

What you'll do

  • Identify which Cyber Kill Chain step each piece of evidence shows
  • Tell attack evidence apart from normal activity
  • Rebuild an attack timeline from logs produced by different tools
  • Choose a control, and its type, that would break the chain at each step
  • Describe one intrusion event with the Diamond Model

The case

The dispatch office incident

Everything below is fictional. Addresses use the ranges reserved for documentation, and domains use example.net.

Kaidō Freight's dispatch office runs on about forty Windows PCs on the 10.20.4.0/24 network, behind a firewall Bastion monitors. Early on Day 9, Bastion's proxy logs flagged an unusually large upload from the dispatch lead's PC, DISP-07 (10.20.4.57). You're the Tier-1 analyst on shift. The Tier-2 investigator has pulled the evidence pack below from six different sources and handed it to you in the order it came off the tools, not in the order things happened.

One artifact is not part of the attack. Real evidence packs always include normal activity that looks suspicious at first glance. Part of your job is to recognize it.

Part 1

Classify the evidence

Decide which Kill Chain step each artifact shows, or mark it "not part of the attack."

  1. Read every artifact once before you classify any of them. Some only make sense next to another one.
  2. For each artifact, write the Kill Chain step number and name. If it isn't part of the attack, write "none."
  3. In the last column, write the specific detail in the artifact that convinced you. "It's suspicious" isn't evidence. "WINWORD.EXE started PowerShell with a hidden, encoded command" is.

Part 2

Rebuild the timeline

Put the attack back in the order it happened.

  1. List the attack artifacts (leave out the one you marked "none") in time order, using their timestamps.
  2. Check that your time order matches the Kill Chain order. Where it doesn't seem to, look again. One artifact's own timestamp is Day 5, but the activity it describes happened earlier.
  3. Calculate the attacker's dwell time: how long from the first successful compromise (exploitation) to when Bastion noticed (the flagged upload).

Part 3

Break the chain

Every step was a chance to stop this attack. Find them.

  1. For each step, name one control that could have stopped or detected it at Kaidō. Be specific to this case: "email filtering" is weaker than "quarantine macro-enabled Office attachments from outside senders."
  2. Give the control's type: preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, recovery, or compensative.
  3. Star (★) the single step where you think breaking the chain would have been cheapest and most effective for Kaidō. You'll defend that choice in the reflection.

Reflection

Write it up

Short answers, in your own words. These aren't auto-graded. This is where you show you understand the attack, not just the labels.

1.Which step did you star in Part 3 as the cheapest and most effective place to break the chain? Defend it, and explain why defenders generally want to stop attacks as early in the chain as possible.

2.The email appliance marked Artifact D "clean," and endpoint protection allowed Artifact A because no signature matched. What does that tell you about relying on signature-based detection? Which Session 5 detection approach would have had a better chance, and why?

3.The C2 beacon in Artifact F ran every 60 seconds for about four days before anyone noticed. What made it hard to spot, and what kind of security data (from Session 12) would have revealed the pattern sooner?

4.Artifact I looked like a lot of data moving at 3 a.m. How did you rule it out? What would have made you treat it as suspicious instead?

5.Now it's Day 9 and you've confirmed this is a true positive. Using the NIST incident response life cycle, list the first three things that should happen next, and say which evidence you'd collect first from DISP-07 according to the order of volatility.
