Overview

What Session 6 covers

Module 10 introduces the cybersecurity cube: a model that describes any security problem along three dimensions. The first is the security principles (the CIA triad from Session 1, now in more depth). The second is the state the data is in. The third is the kind of countermeasure protecting it. Module 11, Understanding Defense, puts the pieces together. Defense in depth layers protections so one failure doesn't expose everything. Operations management keeps watch over those layers through configuration baselines and logs. Policies, BYOD rules, and outside regulations set the rules every layer follows.

  • Explain the three dimensions of the cybersecurity cube
  • Explain how confidentiality, integrity, and availability are protected in practice
  • Compare the three states of data
  • Compare the types of cybersecurity countermeasures
  • Explain how the defense-in-depth strategy is used to protect networks
  • Explain how an organization monitors cybersecurity threats

Concept 1 · Module 10.1

The Cybersecurity Cube

The cybersecurity cube, also called the McCumber Cube, describes data protection along three dimensions. Any one problem sits at one point in each dimension.

DimensionThe question it answersThe three options
Security principlesWhat are we protecting the data for?Confidentiality, integrity, availability (the CIA triad)
States of dataWhere is the data right now?At rest (stored), in transit (moving), in process (being used)
CountermeasuresWhat protects it?Technologies, policies and practices, people
Example of one point on the cube: patient records on Kiriyama General's file server, encrypted so a stolen drive is unreadable. That's confidentiality of data at rest, protected by a technology. Change any one of the three and you need a different defense.
Think of it like… the castle's three questions A castle steward guarding the rice harvest asks the same three things. Why does it matter: so nobody steals it, nobody waters it down, and it's there when the garrison is hungry? Where is it now: in the storehouse, on a cart, or on the counting-house scale? And what guards it: walls and locks, the castle's written rules, or trained guards? Session 1's lord's sealed letters covered the first question. This session adds the other two.

Concept 2 · Module 10.1

Confidentiality in Practice

Confidentiality keeps data away from people, processes, and devices that aren't authorized to see it. The first step is knowing what kind of information you're holding.

Types of sensitive information

TypeWhat it includesBastion example
Personal informationAnything that can identify a person: name, address, medical records, credit records (often called PII, personally identifiable information)Kiriyama General's patient records
Business informationInternal details that give a company its edge: trade secrets, customer lists, contracts, financialsKaidō Freight's customer list and shipping rates
Classified informationGovernment information labeled by sensitivity, such as top secret, secret, or confidentialNone of Bastion's clients. Government agencies and their contractors handle it

Ways to protect it

MethodHow it protects confidentiality
EncryptionScrambles data so only someone with the key can read it
Authentication and access controlVerifies who someone is, then limits what they can open (Session 4's AAA)
TokenizationReplaces sensitive data, like a card number, with a random token. The token has no mathematical link to the original, so it's useless outside the system that issued it
Digital rights management (DRM)Controls how copyrighted digital content, like music, movies, and e-books, can be copied or used
Information rights management (IRM)The same idea applied to an organization's own documents and emails: who can open, forward, print, or edit them
Data anonymizationStrips or alters identifying details so the data can't be traced back to a person, for example before sharing it for research
Tokenization vs. encryption: encrypted data can be turned back into the original by anyone who gets the key. A token can't be reversed at all. Only the system that issued it keeps the lookup table. That's why a stolen token is worthless to an attacker, while stolen encrypted data is only as safe as the key.
Think of it like… a storehouse claim tag Leave goods at the castle storehouse and you get a numbered wooden tag. The tag itself says nothing about what you left, and it's worthless at any other storehouse. Only the storehouse's own book links the number to your goods. A payment token works the same way: steal it, and all you have is a number.

Concept 3 · Module 10.1

Integrity and Availability in Practice

Integrity means data stays accurate, consistent, and trustworthy for its whole life. How hard you work to protect it depends on how much damage bad data would do.

Levels of integrity need

LevelWho needs itWhy
CriticalHealthcare, like Kiriyama GeneralWrong prescription or allergy data can kill someone
HighE-commerce and analytics businessesDecisions and transactions depend on the data, so it's validated and verified often
MidPublic data, like search results and online sales listingsSome verification, and users know to trust it less
LowBlogs and personal social media pagesMostly unverified opinion. Readers shouldn't rely on it

Integrity is protected with access controls (only authorized people can change data), validation checks on what goes in, and hashing, which produces a fingerprint that changes if even one bit of the data changes. Hashing comes back in detail with cryptography in Module 18.

Keeping data available

PracticeWhat it involves
RedundancyNo single point of failure: spare servers, extra network paths, RAID storage (Concept 4)
BackupsCopies of data kept somewhere else. Both the backup system and the backed-up data have to be tested by actually restoring them
Maintenance and patchingPlanned updates so systems don't fail or get knocked offline by a known bug
Disaster planWritten, practiced steps so staff and the security team know how to respond and recover from a major disruption
A backup you've never restored is a hope, not a backup. Ransomware attacks often succeed because the victim's backups turn out to be incomplete, out of date, or encrypted along with everything else.

Concept 4 · Module 10.2

States of Data

Data is always in one of three states, and each state has its own risks. A good defense covers all three.

StateWhat it meansMain risksTypical protection
At restStored and not being used: on a drive, in a database, in the cloudTheft of the device, unauthorized access to the storageDisk and file encryption, access control, physical security
In transitMoving from one device to another: over a cable, over Wi-Fi, or on a carried USB driveInterception, alteration on the way, the transfer being blockedEncryption in transit (like HTTPS or a VPN), mutual authentication
In processBeing input, changed, computed, or outputBad input, corruption, unauthorized changesInput validation, integrity checks, access control on who can modify
Think of it like… the rice harvest The same rice faces different dangers depending on where it is. In the storehouse (at rest), the risk is a thief getting inside, so you need strong doors and a guard on the key. On a cart on the highway (in transit), bandits can stop it or swap it, so you need escorts and sealed bales. On the counting-house scale (in process), a dishonest clerk can misweigh it, so you need careful records and a second person checking.

Data at rest: where it's stored

Storage typeWhat it isSecurity notes
Direct-attached storage (DAS)Storage connected to one computer: an internal drive or a plugged-in USB driveNot shared by default. Hard to manage at scale, and only as safe as the one host it's attached to
RAIDRedundant array of independent disks: several drives that the system treats as oneImproves performance and fault tolerance (a drive can fail without losing data). RAID is not a backup: deleted or encrypted files are gone on every disk at once
Network-attached storage (NAS)A storage device on the network that many authorized users can save to and read fromCentralized and easy to back up, but it's a single, valuable target
Storage area network (SAN)A dedicated high-speed network connecting servers to shared storageBuilt for performance and scale in data centers. Needs its own access controls
Cloud storageStorage in a provider's data center, reached over the internetSomeone else runs the hardware, but you're still responsible for who can access your data (Module 17)

Data in transit and in process

Data in transit moves over wired networks, over wireless, or by sneakernet: someone physically carrying it on removable media like a USB drive. Each path needs protecting. Mutual authentication, where the client and the server each verify the other's identity, stops a device from connecting to an impostor such as Session 3's evil twin access point.

Data in process goes through three stages: input (typing, forms, file uploads, sensors), modification (manual edits or automated processing, like compression or encryption), and output (to a screen, a printer, or another system). Data corruption, accidental or deliberate, can happen at any of those stages and leave data unreadable or wrong.

Concept 5 · Module 10.3

Countermeasures: Technologies

The third dimension of the cube is what actually protects the data. Technology is the most visible kind of countermeasure, and most of it is either software running on a host or hardware sitting on the network.

TypeExamplesWhat they do
Software-basedSoftware firewalls, network and port scanners, protocol analyzers, vulnerability scanners, host-based IDSControl traffic to one host, find open ports, capture and examine traffic, find weaknesses, and watch a host for suspicious activity
Hardware-basedFirewall appliances, IDS/IPS appliances, proxy servers, biometric access controlFilter or block traffic for a whole network, stand between users and the internet, and verify identity physically

Tools you'll see named on the exam

ToolWhat it's for
Port scannerFinds which ports are open on a host, so you can spot services that shouldn't be listening
Protocol analyzerCaptures and decodes traffic. It's how you learn what normal traffic looks like (a baseline) and find misconfigurations. Wireshark is one
Vulnerability scannerChecks systems against a list of known weaknesses and reports what needs fixing
Proxy serverMakes requests to the internet on users' behalf, so the outside world sees one address instead of each user's
Biometric access controlVerifies identity with a physical trait, like a fingerprint or face, before granting access
Firewalls, IDS, and IPS were covered in Session 4. Host-based firewalls and HIDS were covered in Session 5.

Concept 6 · Module 10.3

Countermeasures: People and Policies

Technology fails if nobody knows how to use it or what the rules are. The other two countermeasures are trained people and written policies.

People: a security awareness program teaches every employee, not just IT, to recognize threats like phishing and to follow security procedures. Awareness only works when it's ongoing and when it's backed by clear policies.

The security policy

A security policy is a set of written security goals and rules for an organization. It shows the organization's commitment to security and sets the rules for how people behave. Most policies include these parts:

PolicyWhat it covers
Identification and authenticationWho is allowed to access network resources, and how their identity is verified
Password policyPassword strength and how often passwords must change
Acceptable use policy (AUP)What users may and may not do on the organization's systems, such as banned websites or apps that hog bandwidth
Remote access policyHow off-site users, like Kaidō Freight's drivers on VPN, may connect
Network maintenance policyHow and when operating systems and applications get updated
Incident handling policyThe procedures for responding to a security incident

Standards, guidelines, and procedures

DocumentMandatory?Example
StandardYes. A required rule everyone follows the same way"All laptops use full-disk encryption."
GuidelineNo. A recommendation for doing something more securely"Consider using a password manager."
ProcedureYes. Step-by-step instructions, often with screenshots, for doing a task the approved way"How to enable BitLocker: step 1, open Control Panel…"

Organizations don't have to write guidelines from scratch. Outside sources include NIST (the US National Institute of Standards and Technology), the NSA's security configuration guides, and the Common Criteria, an international standard for evaluating the security of IT products.

Think of it like… walls, laws, and guards A castle needs all three. Walls and gates (technology) do nothing if the gate is left open. The castle's written rules (policies) say who may pass and when, but rules nobody reads protect nothing. Trained guards (people) make the rules and the walls work. Take away any one and the other two have a gap an attacker can walk through.

Concept 7 · Module 11.1

Assets, Vulnerabilities, and Threats

Your job is to secure the organization's assets. Before you can plan any defense, you have to know three things: what you're protecting, where it's weak, and what could go wrong.

TermDefinitionKaidō Freight example
AssetAnything of value to the organization that must be protected: servers, infrastructure devices, end devices, and the greatest asset, dataThe domain controller, the routing database, the drivers' tablets
VulnerabilityA weakness in a system or its design that a threat actor could exploitA VPN appliance that's three patches behind
ThreatAny potential danger to an assetA ransomware gang scanning the internet for that VPN flaw

Identify assets

Every device and every piece of information the organization owns or manages is an asset. Together they make up the attack surface (Session 1's term). As the organization grows, so does the list. Asset management means inventorying all of those assets, then developing and implementing policies and procedures to protect them. You also need to know where the critical information is stored and how people get to it.

Asset classification sorts assets into groups with common characteristics, so the most critical information gets the most protection. A labeling system shows how valuable, sensitive, and critical each item is. It takes four steps:

StepWhat you do
1. Choose the categoryInformation assets, software assets, physical assets, or services
2. Establish accountabilityName an owner for every information asset and every piece of application software
3. Set the criteriaClassify by confidentiality, value, time, access rights, and destruction
4. Apply a schemaLabel information the same way everywhere, so protection is uniform and monitoring is easier

Asset standardization picks the specific hardware and software products the organization uses. When something fails, a standard part can be swapped in fast. A non-standard environment needs more expertise to run and costs more in maintenance contracts and spare inventory.

The asset lifecycle

StageWhat happens
ProcurementThe organization buys the asset based on a documented need, and it's added to inventory
DeploymentStaff inspect and test it, then tag it (a label or barcode) for tracking. It moves from inventory to in use
UtilizationThe longest stage. Performance is checked continuously, and upgrades, patches, license renewals, and compliance audits happen here
MaintenanceModifications and upgrades that extend the asset's productive life
DisposalAll data is wiped first. The asset may be dismantled for parts, and hazardous parts go out under local guidelines

Identify vulnerabilities and threats

Threat identification produces a list of the threats likely in your environment. Ask three questions about each system. What are its possible vulnerabilities? Who might want to exploit them to reach specific information assets? What are the consequences if they do and the assets are lost?

NetAcad's example is an online banking system. Its threat list would include:

ThreatWhat it looks like
Internal system compromiseThe attacker uses the exposed e-banking servers to break into internal bank systems
Stolen customer dataCustomers' personal and financial data is taken from the customer database
Phony transactions from an external serverThe attacker alters the application's code and makes transactions while impersonating a real user
Phony transactions with a stolen PIN or smart cardThe attacker steals a customer's identity and uses the account
Insider attackAn employee finds a flaw and uses it to mount an attack
Data input errorsA user enters wrong data or makes incorrect transaction requests
Data center destructionA disaster severely damages or destroys the data center

Notice that the last two aren't attacks at all. Threat identification covers anything that could cost you the asset, including mistakes and disasters.

Concept 8 · Module 11.1

Defense in Depth

Defense in depth uses multiple layers of security at the network edge, inside the network, and on the endpoints. The layers work together, so the failure of one safeguard doesn't weaken the others.

Line of defenseWhat it does
1. Edge routerApplies rules that allow or deny traffic, and passes connections meant for the internal LAN to the firewall
2. FirewallA checkpoint device that does additional filtering and tracks the state of each connection
3. Internal routerApplies final filtering rules before traffic reaches its destination

Routers and firewalls aren't the only layers. IPS, Advanced Malware Protection (AMP), web and email content security, identity services, and network access control (Session 4's NAC) all add more.

Security onion vs. security artichoke

AnalogyThe idea
Security onionA threat actor has to peel away the network's defenses layer by layer. Only after getting through every layer do they reach the target data or system
Security artichokeBorderless networks changed the picture, in the attacker's favor. Threat actors only need to remove certain "leaves," and each leaf may reveal sensitive data that isn't well secured. They chip away at the perimeter to reach the heart of the enterprise

Don't confuse the security onion analogy with Security Onion, a suite of network security monitoring tools. Same name, different thing.

Defense-in-depth strategies

StrategyWhat it means
LayeringMultiple defenses that work together as one barrier
LimitingEach user gets only the access their job requires. Less access means fewer chances for a threat
DiversityThe layers are different from each other, so a technique that gets through one doesn't get through all of them
ObscurityDon't reveal information attackers can use, like which OS a server runs or the make of its equipment
SimplicityComplexity doesn't guarantee security. A setup people can't configure correctly is as easy to break as a weak one
Takeaway: the artichoke doesn't make layers useless. It means you can't assume every attack starts at the outer edge. Devices and data that live outside the perimeter need protection of their own.
Think of it like… the castle's rings A castle is built in rings: a moat, an outer bailey, an inner bailey, and finally the keep. Losing the outer wall isn't losing the castle, because each ring has to be taken separately. That's the onion. Here's where the picture breaks, and why the artichoke replaced it: a retainer carrying documents outside the walls can be robbed on the road without anyone crossing a single ring. A Kaidō Freight driver's tablet on a highway rest stop's Wi-Fi is that retainer.

Concept 9 · Module 11.2

Cybersecurity Operations Management

Layers only help if someone is watching them. This topic covers how an organization keeps track of what its systems should look like, and records what actually happens on them.

Configuration management

Configuration management is identifying, controlling, and auditing a system's established baseline and every change made to it. The baseline configuration is all the settings configured for a system. It acts as a template for every similar system. Documented configuration resources can include:

  • Network maps, cabling and wiring diagrams, and application configuration specifications
  • Standard naming conventions for computers
  • An IP schema that tracks IP addresses

Hardening the OS is a big part of a secure configuration: set up log files and auditing, change default account names and passwords, and apply account policies and file-level access control. Session 5 covered these as operating system security.

Log files and log management

A log records events as they happen. Each log entry holds all the information about one event, and the entries together make up a log file. Security generates a lot of logs, so organizations need a log management process. It sets the procedures for:

ProcedureThe question it answers
GeneratingWhich events get logged?
TransmittingHow do logs get to where they're kept?
StoringWhere are they kept, and for how long?
AnalyzingWho reviews them, and how?
DisposingHow are old logs safely removed?
Log typeWhat it records
Operating system logsEvents tied to the OS, such as client requests and server responses (like successful user authentications) and usage information (the number and size of transactions over time)
Application security logsOutput from network-based or system-based security software that detects malicious activity

Logs are useful for auditing and for spotting trends and long-term problems. They also give the organization documentation that it complies with laws and regulations.

Protocol analyzers

A protocol analyzer (also called a packet analyzer or packet sniffer) intercepts and logs network traffic, wired or wireless. It captures each packet, reads its fields, and analyzes the contents. Wireshark and tcpdump are two examples. Analysts use them for:

  • Traffic logging
  • Network problem analysis
  • Detecting network misuse
  • Detecting intrusion attempts
  • Isolating exploited systems
Protocol analyzers first appeared in Concept 5. Syslog, which gathers logs from many devices into one place, is in Session 4. Linux log files like /var/log/syslog are in Session 5.

Concept 10 · Module 11.3

Security Policies, Regulations, and Standards

Business policies define correct behavior for the business and its employees. That gives you a baseline: if behavior that breaks policy shows up on the network, a breach may have happened.

Business policies

PolicyWhat it covers
Company policiesThe rules of conduct and the responsibilities of both employees and employers. They protect workers' rights and the employer's business interests
Employee policiesCreated and kept by human resources: salary, pay schedule, benefits, work schedule, vacations, and more. New employees usually review and sign them
Security policiesThe security objectives, the rules of behavior for users and administrators, and the system requirements. The parts are in Concept 6: identification and authentication, passwords, acceptable use, remote access, network maintenance, and incident handling

A comprehensive security policy:

  • Demonstrates the organization's commitment to security
  • Sets the rules for expected behavior
  • Keeps system operations, hardware and software purchasing and use, and maintenance consistent
  • Defines the legal consequences of violations
  • Gives security staff the backing of management

It also provides a baseline for acquiring, configuring, and auditing systems for compliance.

BYOD policies

Bring your own device (BYOD) lets employees use their own mobile devices to reach company systems, software, networks, or information. Organizations get higher productivity, lower IT and operating costs, more mobile employees, and an edge in hiring and keeping staff. A BYOD security policy should:

  • Specify the goals of the BYOD program
  • Identify which employees can bring their own devices, and which devices are supported
  • Identify the level of access employees get on personal devices
  • Describe what security personnel may access and do on the device
  • Identify which regulations apply when employee devices are used
  • Identify safeguards for when a device is compromised

BYOD security best practices

PracticeWhat it means
Password-protected accessUnique passwords for each device and account
Manually control wireless connectivityTurn off Wi-Fi and Bluetooth when not in use, and connect only to trusted networks
Keep updatedKeep the OS and other software current. Updates often carry patches for the latest threats
Back up dataEnable backups in case the device is lost or stolen
Enable "Find my Device"Subscribe to a device locator service that can remotely wipe the device
Provide antivirus softwareProvide antivirus for approved BYOD devices
Use mobile device management (MDM) softwareLets IT apply security settings and software configurations to every device that connects to the company network

Regulatory and standards compliance

Some rules come from outside the organization. Network security professionals need to know the laws and the codes of ethics that bind information systems security (INFOSEC) professionals. Many organizations are required to develop and implement security policies. Compliance regulations define what the organization must provide and its liability if it doesn't. Which regulations apply depends on the type of organization and the data it handles. Two examples among Bastion's clients:

RequirementWho it applies toBastion client
Patient-privacy law (HIPAA in the US)Healthcare organizations that hold patient recordsKiriyama General Hospital
PCI DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard)Any business that accepts, processes, or stores payment cardsTsukimi Sweets, at its register
Governance and compliance get a full session later in the course. For now, know that compliance is set from outside, and the organization's own policies have to meet it.

Confidentiality

The card numbers at the register

Tsukimi Sweets is replacing its point-of-sale terminal after a card-skimming scare. The vendor offers two options: encrypt card numbers and store them in the shop's database, or tokenize them so the shop's database only ever holds tokens. Which better protects the shop if its database is stolen, and why?

Show answer

Tokenization. A stolen token can't be turned back into a card number, because the token has no mathematical link to it. Only the payment processor's system can map a token to a card.

Why not encryption: encrypted card numbers are only as safe as the key. If the attacker gets the database and the key, which are often on the same server, the numbers can be recovered.

Integrity

How much checking is enough?

Kiriyama General's pharmacy system and the hospital's public "news and events" blog both get a request to drop a validation step to speed things up. Using the levels of integrity need, how should you answer each?

Show answer

Pharmacy system: critical integrity need. A wrong dose or a missing allergy can kill a patient, so every validation step stays.

Public blog: low integrity need. A typo in an event post is embarrassing, not dangerous, so lighter checking is reasonable. (It still needs access control, so nobody can deface it.)

States of data

Name the state

At Kaidō Freight, three things happen in one minute: (1) a dispatcher types a delivery address into the routing app, (2) the routing app sends the finished route to a driver's tablet over the cellular network, and (3) last month's routes sit in the database on the file server. Which state is each in?

Show answer

(1) In process: the input stage.

(2) In transit: moving between devices, so it needs encryption on the way.

(3) At rest: stored and not being used.

Storage

The contracts on one PC

Kaidō Freight's shipping contracts live on the internal hard drive of one office manager's PC. Other staff get copies by asking the manager to email them or by carrying them over on a USB stick. What storage type is that, what are the risks, and what would you suggest instead?

Show answer

Storage type: direct-attached storage (DAS). The data is only as safe as that one PC, and there's likely no central backup.

Transit risk: the USB copies are sneakernet. They can be lost, stolen, or carry malware between machines, and nobody tracks where the copies end up.

Better option: a network-attached storage (NAS) device or a managed file server, with access control on the contracts folder and regular, tested backups.

Policies

Standard, guideline, or procedure?

Three lines from Kiriyama General's security documents: (A) "Staff should lock their screen whenever they step away." (B) "All workstations must lock automatically after 5 minutes of inactivity." (C) "To set the lock timer: open Settings, choose Accounts, then Sign-in options…"

Show answer

(A) Guideline: a recommendation, worded as "should."

(B) Standard: a mandatory rule, worded as "must."

(C) Procedure: step-by-step instructions for doing the task.

The cube

Place it on the cube

Kaidō Freight runs a monthly phishing-awareness session for drivers, so they stop opening fake "delivery exception" emails on their tablets. Where does this fall on each of the cube's three dimensions?

Show answer

Countermeasure: people (awareness training).

Principle: mainly confidentiality (stopping stolen credentials), though a phish that installs malware could hit integrity and availability too.

State of data: the credentials are at risk in process, at the moment the driver types them into a fake page.

Defense in depth

"We have a firewall now"

Kaidō Freight just installed a new firewall. Their IT manager says, "Since we have the firewall, we can skip patching the internal file server this quarter." Using defense in depth, how do you answer?

Show answer

The firewall is one layer, not the whole defense. If a phishing email lands malware on an office PC, the attacker is already inside the firewall. The unpatched file server is then the next easy step.

The artichoke view makes it worse: drivers connect over VPN from their tablets. A stolen tablet or driver password skips the outer layers entirely.

Answer: patch the server. Each layer covers for the others' failures, and the patch closes a known vulnerability on an asset the firewall can't fully protect.

BYOD

Orders on personal phones

Tsukimi Sweets' owner wants the counter staff to watch the online order queue on their own phones. What should a basic BYOD policy for the shop cover?

Show answer

Who and what: which staff may do it, and which phones are supported.

Access and data: the phones see the order queue only. Card numbers never go on a personal phone, because PCI DSS applies to the shop.

Device rules: a passcode, current updates, and "Find My Device" turned on.

Lost device and leaving: how to report a lost phone, and how the owner removes a departing employee's access to the order queue the day they leave.

Operations management

The account nobody remembers

During a routine check of Kaidō Freight's Linux web server, you find a local account named svc-backup2 with sudo rights. Nobody on the IT team remembers creating it. Which two operations-management practices from Module 11.2 help you decide whether it's a problem, and how?

Show answer

Configuration management: compare the server against its documented baseline configuration. If svc-backup2 isn't in the baseline and there's no approved change for it, it's an unauthorized change. Treat it as suspicious until someone proves otherwise.

Log management: the server's operating system logs should show when the account was created, from which session, and every time it has signed in since. If the logs were sent to a central server (Session 4's syslog), an attacker who deleted the local copies didn't erase the record.
