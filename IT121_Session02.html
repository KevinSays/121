Overview

What Session 2 covers

Two modules, one throughline: first, how a network is organized and defended at a structural level (domains, zones, policy); then, how the foundational protocols everything else runs on top of — IP, TCP/UDP, ARP, DNS, DHCP — get attacked. None of these protocols were designed with an attacker in mind, and that gap is the whole story.

  • Name the major cybersecurity domains and what each one is responsible for
  • Explain security zones (trusted, untrusted, DMZ) and defense in depth
  • Distinguish a Business Continuity Plan from a Disaster Recovery Plan, and RTO from RPO
  • Explain how IP, ICMP, TCP, and UDP get abused — spoofing, floods, amplification, hijacking
  • Identify ARP, DNS, and DHCP attacks and why each protocol is vulnerable by design

Concept 1

Security Domains

"Cybersecurity" isn't one job — it's several overlapping specialties. An alert doesn't announce which domain it belongs to; figuring that out is half the work.

DomainWhat it coversExample control
Network securityThe network itself — traffic, segmentation, the perimeterFirewalls, VPNs, network segmentation
Endpoint securityIndividual devices — laptops, servers, mobileAntivirus/EDR, patch management
Application securitySoftware, before it ever reaches a networkSecure coding, input validation, WAFs
Data securityData at rest, in transit, and in useEncryption, DLP, backups
Identity & access management (IAM)Who can do what, and proving itMFA, least privilege, access reviews
Cloud securityWorkloads and data hosted off-premisesShared responsibility model, cloud IAM
A SOC analyst rotates across most of these in a single shift. A phishing email touches identity and endpoint; a misconfigured S3 bucket touches cloud and data. Real incidents rarely respect the boundary between domains.

Concept 2

Security Zones & Defense in Depth

No single control stops every attack, so real networks layer several — and separate what's exposed to the internet from what isn't.

ZoneTrust levelExample
UntrustedNoneThe internet
DMZ (demilitarized zone)Semi-trustedPublic web server, mail relay
TrustedHighInternal LAN, employee workstations
A DMZ exists so that if a public-facing server gets popped, the attacker lands in a fenced yard, not the living room. It's isolated from the trusted internal network even though it's reachable from the untrusted one.

Concept 3

Security Policies & Business Continuity

Controls are technical; policy is the paperwork that says what's allowed and what happens when something breaks anyway.

Policy / planPurpose
Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)Defines what employees are and aren't allowed to do with company systems
Incident response policyWhat happens the moment something goes wrong
Remote access policyRules for connecting to the network from outside it
Business Continuity Plan (BCP)Keeps critical operations running during a disruption
Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP)Restores systems and data after a disruption — a subset of BCP

Two numbers every recovery plan needs

  • RTO (Recovery Time Objective) — how long can we be down before it's unacceptable?
  • RPO (Recovery Point Objective) — how much recent data can we afford to lose?
BCP keeps the business running; DRP gets the systems back. A hospital's BCP might mean switching to paper charts during an outage, while its DRP is the separate process of actually restoring the EHR.

Concept 4 · Module 3

IP Vulnerabilities & Attacks

IP has no built-in way to verify a source address. Everything in this section exploits that one trust gap.

AttackHow it works
IP spoofingForges the source IP address so traffic — or the blame for it — appears to come from somewhere else
ICMP reconnaissancePing sweeps map which hosts are alive before an attack even starts
ICMP redirectA forged redirect message tricks a host into routing its traffic through an attacker-controlled path
Amplification & reflectionA small spoofed request (e.g. to a DNS or NTP server) triggers a huge response — sent to the victim, not the attacker
Spoofing is the common thread. Once the source address can't be trusted, an attacker can hide behind it, redirect blame to an innocent host, or turn a third-party server into an unwitting weapon.

Concept 5

TCP & UDP Vulnerabilities

TCP's handshake is a feature for reliability and a liability for availability — every half-open connection ties up a resource somewhere.

AttackHow it works
SYN floodOpens thousands of connections and never completes the handshake, until there's no room left for real ones
TCP reset attackA forged RST packet kills a legitimate, already-established connection
TCP session hijackingPredicts or steals sequence numbers to take over a session someone else already authenticated into
UDP floodOverwhelms a target with connectionless UDP traffic — cheap to send, expensive for the target to process

Concept 6

ARP, DNS & DHCP Attacks

ARP, DNS, and DHCP all exist to make networking convenient. None of them require proof of identity by default — which is exactly what these attacks abuse.

AttackHow it works
ARP spoofing (poisoning)Fake ARP replies redirect local traffic through the attacker — a classic man-in-the-middle
DNS cache poisoningInjects a false name-to-address mapping into a resolver's cache
DNS tunnelingSmuggles data inside DNS queries and responses, often past firewalls that implicitly trust DNS traffic
DHCP starvationExhausts a DHCP server's address pool with a flood of fake lease requests
DHCP spoofing (rogue DHCP)An attacker's own DHCP server answers first, handing out a malicious gateway or DNS server
Notice the pattern: ARP trusts the last reply it heard, DNS resolvers trust the first response that beats the real one back, and DHCP clients trust whichever server answers fastest. Speed, not identity, wins by default in all three.

Zones

Which zone, and why?

A university publishes a web server that's reachable from the internet, but it sits on its own isolated segment with no direct path to the internal grading and registration systems.

Show answer

Answer: That segment is a DMZ — semi-trusted, reachable from the untrusted internet, but deliberately isolated from the trusted internal network. If the web server is compromised, the attacker doesn't automatically reach grading or registration.

TCP/UDP

Diagnose the logs

A server's connection logs show thousands of sessions stuck in a half-open state — SYN received, but the handshake never completes. Legitimate users can't connect.

Show answer

Answer: SYN flood. The attacker sends SYNs (often with spoofed source addresses) and never sends the final ACK, so every half-open connection sits in the server's connection table until it's full — starving out real traffic.

ARP vs. DNS

Same symptom, different cause

Scenario A: everyone on the third floor suddenly has their traffic silently rerouted through a laptop that just joined the network. Scenario B: employees type the correct company URL but land on a fake login page.

Show answer

Scenario A: ARP spoofing — a local device sent fake ARP replies to reroute traffic through itself.

Scenario B: DNS cache poisoning — a false name-to-address mapping sent visitors to the wrong (attacker-controlled) server, even though they typed the right name.

BCP vs. DRP

Which plan is which?

During a ransomware outage, a hospital immediately switches to paper charts so patient care can continue. Separately, its IT team works to restore the electronic health record system from backups.

Show answer

Paper charts: Business Continuity Plan (BCP) — keeping critical operations (patient care) running during the disruption.

Restoring the EHR: Disaster Recovery Plan (DRP) — the technical process of getting systems and data back.

RTO vs. RPO

Name the number

A company's disaster recovery documentation states two limits: the backup system can restore data from no more than 4 hours before an incident, and critical systems must be back online within 1 hour of an outage being declared.

Show answer

4 hours of acceptable data loss: RPO (Recovery Point Objective).

1 hour to be back online: RTO (Recovery Time Objective).

RPO looks backward at how much you can afford to lose; RTO looks forward at how fast you have to recover.
