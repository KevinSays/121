Almost every security control in this course leans on cryptography somewhere: the VPN between two clouds, the hash in an integrity check, the padlock in the browser bar. This session opens the box. It covers how data is kept secret, how you prove it wasn't changed and who sent it, and the system of certificates that lets strangers on the internet trust each other at all.

Overview

What Session 11 covers

Module 18 is the whole cryptography toolkit in one module. It starts with encryption for confidentiality and other ways to hide data, moves to hashing for integrity and authenticity, then to public key cryptography, digital signatures, and the public key infrastructure (PKI) that makes certificates trustworthy. It ends with where all of this shows up in practice, and why encryption makes a security analyst's job harder as well as easier.

  • Explain how to use hashing tools
  • Use a digital signature
  • Use hashing to detect network interception

The four goals of secure communication. Keep these in mind for the whole session. Each tool below serves one or more of them. Confidentiality: only the intended people can read it. Integrity: it wasn't changed on the way. Origin authentication: it really came from who it says. Nonrepudiation: the sender can't later deny sending it.

Concept 1 · Module 18.1

Confidentiality

Encryption turns readable data into something only the right key can turn back.

Encryption basics

Encryption uses an algorithm, called a cipher, and a key to turn readable plaintext into unreadable ciphertext. Decryption reverses it. The algorithms are public. The security comes from keeping the key secret and making it long enough that guessing every possible key, a brute-force attack, would take impossibly long. The study of making codes is cryptography; the study of breaking them is cryptanalysis.

Symmetric and asymmetric encryption

Symmetric encryption uses the same key to encrypt and decrypt. Both sides must already share that key, a pre-shared key. It's fast, so it's used for bulk data such as VPN traffic. Its weak point is key management: how do you get the key to the other side safely, and how many keys do you need when many people talk to each other?

Asymmetric encryption uses a key pair: a public key anyone may have and a private key only the owner holds. Whatever one key encrypts, only the other key can decrypt. It solves the key-sharing problem, but it's much slower, so it's used for small jobs: exchanging keys, digital signatures, and certificates.

Symmetric algorithms

AlgorithmKey lengthNotes
DES56 bitsData Encryption Standard. Legacy and easily broken today. Avoid it
3DES112 or 168 bitsTriple DES: runs DES three times. Stronger, but slow and being retired
AES128, 192, or 256 bitsAdvanced Encryption Standard. Secure, efficient, and the recommended choice
SEAL160 bitsSoftware-Optimized Encryption Algorithm. A fast stream cipher with low CPU cost
RC seriesVariesRivest ciphers RC2, RC4, RC5, RC6. RC4 was widely used in old Wi-Fi (WEP) and SSL and is now considered broken

Symmetric ciphers work in one of two ways. A block cipher encrypts fixed-size blocks of data at a time (AES uses 128-bit blocks; DES uses 64-bit). A stream cipher encrypts one bit or byte at a time (SEAL and RC4), which suits data that arrives continuously.

Asymmetric algorithms

AlgorithmTypical key lengthUsed for
Diffie-Hellman (DH)512 – 4096 bitsLets two parties agree on a shared secret key over an untrusted network without ever sending the key
RSA512 – 4096 bitsEncryption and digital signatures. Based on the difficulty of factoring very large numbers
DSA / DSS512 – 1024 bits (larger today)Digital Signature Algorithm, defined in the Digital Signature Standard. Signatures only, not encryption
ElGamal512 – 1024 bitsEncryption and key exchange, built on Diffie-Hellman
Elliptic curve (ECC)160 bits and upSame jobs with much shorter keys, so it's popular on phones and small devices

Asymmetric keys look far longer than symmetric ones, but the numbers aren't comparable. A 2048-bit RSA key gives roughly the strength of a 112-bit symmetric key, because the math behind RSA has shortcuts that brute force on AES doesn't.

Choosing: the two work together

Real protocols use both. Diffie-Hellman (or RSA) safely establishes a shared secret, then fast AES encrypts the actual data with it. HTTPS, SSH, and IPsec VPNs all work this way. DH strength is chosen as a DH group: groups 1, 2, and 5 are too weak and shouldn't be used, while groups 14 and up (and the elliptic curve groups 19, 20, and 24) are current choices.

The key-sharing problem is the heart of this section, and it's older than computers. Anyone who's tried to get a secret to someone far away has run into it. Here's how it looks in the castle town.

Think of it like… the courier's locked box A merchant in the castle town sends letters to a partner in Edo by courier, in a box with a lock. If both own a copy of the same key, that's symmetric: quick and simple, but the key has to be carried there first, and a courier who copies it can read everything after. Now imagine the partner sends ahead a stack of open padlocks that only the partner's own key opens. Anyone can snap one shut on a box, but no one, not even the sender, can open it again except the partner. That's asymmetric: the open padlocks are the public key, and the one key that opens them is the private key. Snapping padlocks is slow work, so in practice the merchant locks one small box holding a fresh shared key, and uses that key for the rest of the season's letters. That's DH or RSA followed by AES.

Concept 2 · Module 18.2

Obscuring Data

Encryption isn't the only way to protect data. Sometimes you hide it, disguise it, or make it confusing on purpose.

Data maskingReplaces sensitive data with realistic but fake values, so the data can be used (for testing, training, or analysis) without exposing the real information. Common techniques: substitution (swap real values for fake ones that look right) and shuffling (mix real values between records, so no row is a real person).
SteganographyHides a message inside another file, such as an image, audio file, or video, so nobody knows a message exists at all. A common method changes the least significant bit of each pixel's color values, which changes the image too little for the eye to notice.
Social steganographyHides a message in plain sight by writing something that means one thing to everyone and something else to a specific audience: inside jokes, slang, or references only the intended readers understand.
SteganalysisDetecting that hidden information exists in a file. It looks for statistical oddities, file sizes that don't fit the content, or differences from a known original.
ObfuscationMaking data or code deliberately confusing so it's hard to understand, without hiding that it exists. Software developers obfuscate code to protect it from copying and reverse engineering. Attackers obfuscate malware to slip past antivirus.
Encryption vs. steganography. Encryption hides what a message says, but everyone can see that there is a secret. Steganography hides that there is a message at all. They can be combined: encrypt the message, then hide the ciphertext in a picture.
Data states, and why test environments shouldn't hold live data, are in Session 10. Malware that hides from antivirus is in Session 5.

Concept 3 · Module 18.3

Integrity and Authenticity

A hash proves data didn't change. Add a secret key and it also proves who sent it.

Cryptographic hash functions

A hash function takes data of any size and produces a short, fixed-length value called a hash (also a digest or fingerprint). A cryptographic hash function has these properties:

  • The input can be any length.
  • The output is always a fixed length.
  • It's relatively easy to compute for any input.
  • It's one way: you can't work backward from the hash to the input.
  • It's collision free: it's computationally infeasible to find two different inputs with the same hash.

Change one bit of the input and roughly half the output bits change. This is called the avalanche effect. Hashing isn't encryption: there is no key and nothing to decrypt.

Hash algorithms

AlgorithmHash lengthStatus
MD5128 bitsLegacy. Collisions can be created on purpose, so don't use it for security. Still fine for spotting accidental corruption
SHA-1160 bitsLegacy. Practical collisions have been demonstrated. Avoid
SHA-2224, 256, 384, or 512 bitsCurrent standard. SHA-256 is the most common. Use it
SHA-3224, 256, 384, or 512 bitsNewest standard, built on a different design than SHA-2. A strong alternative

Why a plain hash isn't enough: HMAC

A hash proves integrity, but only if you trust where the hash came from. An attacker in the middle can change a message, compute a fresh hash for the altered version, and send both. The receiver's check passes. A hash alone gives integrity against accidents, not against attackers.

A keyed-hash message authentication code (HMAC) fixes that by mixing a secret key into the hash. Only someone holding the key can produce the right HMAC. The receiver, who also has the key, recomputes it and compares. A match proves both integrity and origin authentication. HMAC is named after its hash: HMAC-SHA-256, for example. IPsec VPNs and routing protocol authentication use it.

Integrity checks and checksums are in Session 7. HMAC as an authentication method is in Session 7's accounts section.

Concept 4 · Module 18.4

Hashing

Hashing tools in practice: checking files, storing passwords, and the attacks against both.

Hashing tools

Every operating system can compute a hash from the command line:

PlatformCommand
Windows (PowerShell)Get-FileHash .\file.exe (SHA-256 by default; add -Algorithm MD5 or another to change it)
Linuxsha256sum file, md5sum file
macOSshasum -a 256 file

Two common uses: comparing a download's hash to the one its publisher posted, to confirm you got the real, unchanged file; and recording the hash of evidence when it's collected, so you can prove later that it wasn't altered. Security tools also look up file hashes against databases of known malware.

Hashing passwords

Systems shouldn't store passwords. They store the hash of each password. At login, the system hashes what you typed and compares the two hashes. If the password database is stolen, the attacker gets hashes, not passwords, and has to crack them by guessing:

Dictionary attackHashes every word in a list of common passwords and words, and looks for a matching hash. Fast against weak passwords.
Brute-force attackTries every possible combination of characters. Given enough time it will always succeed, so the defense is making it take impossibly long.
Rainbow tablesHuge precomputed tables of passwords and their hashes. Instead of hashing guesses, the attacker just looks the stolen hash up.

Salting

A salt is a random value, unique to each user, added to the password before it's hashed and stored alongside the hash. Two users with the same password now get different hashes. Precomputed rainbow tables become useless, because the attacker would need a separate table for every possible salt. Salting is why a good password database leak exposes far less than a bad one. Purpose-built password hashing functions such as bcrypt, scrypt, and Argon2 add salting automatically and are deliberately slow, to make cracking even harder.

Concept 5 · Module 18.5

Public Key Cryptography

The same key pair gives you two different tools, depending on which key you lock with.

Two ways to use a key pair

For confidentialityEncrypt with the receiver's public key. Only the receiver's private key can decrypt it, so only the receiver can read it.
For authenticationEncrypt with the sender's private key. Anyone can decrypt it with the sender's public key, so it isn't secret, but a successful decryption proves only the sender could have produced it.

Digital signatures

A digital signature uses the authentication direction. Encrypting a whole document with a private key would be slow, so the sender signs a hash of it instead:

A valid signature provides three things:

AuthenticityProves the data was signed by the holder of that private key.
IntegrityProves the data hasn't changed since it was signed. Any change breaks the hash match.
NonrepudiationA third party can accept the signature as proof the exchange happened. The signer can't credibly deny signing, because only they hold the private key.

Digital signatures are commonly built with DSA or RSA. Two everyday uses:

  • Code signing: the software publisher signs its programs and updates. Your computer checks the signature before installing, which confirms the code is from that publisher and wasn't tampered with since.
  • Digital certificates: a certificate is itself a document signed by a trusted authority, binding a name to a public key. That's the next section.

Signatures raise a question the math alone can't answer. If you check a signature with "the sender's public key," how do you know that key really belongs to the sender and not to an impostor who handed you theirs? People settled a version of that problem long ago, with seals.

Think of it like… the merchant's registered seal Edo-period merchants signed contracts with a carved personal seal, a hanko, pressed in red ink. Only the owner held the seal, so the impression proved who agreed. That's the private key making a signature. But a stranger can't tell a real seal from a forgery by looking. So important seals were recorded in an official seal register kept by the town and village authorities. A buyer in doubt could check the impression against the register. The register is the certificate authority, and the recorded impression is the certificate: a trusted third party vouching that this mark belongs to this person. Japan still registers seals this way today.

Concept 6 · Module 18.6

Authorities and the PKI Trust System

Public key infrastructure is how strangers decide whose public key to believe.

What a PKI is

A public key infrastructure (PKI) is the framework of hardware, software, people, policies, and procedures needed to create, manage, store, distribute, and revoke digital certificates. Its core pieces:

Certificate authority (CA)The trusted third party that verifies identities and issues certificates, signing each one with its own private key. Like a government office issuing ID cards.
Digital certificateAn electronic document binding an identity (a person, server, or device) to a public key, signed by the CA. Certificates are public information. The standard format is X.509.
Registration authority (RA)A subordinate that handles enrollment requests and verifies identity for the CA, so the CA doesn't have to do every check itself.
Certificate storeThe trusted root CA certificates built into your OS and browser. Your device trusts any certificate that chains back to one of them.

How trust works

Your browser doesn't know your bank's public key in advance. The bank's server sends its certificate, and your browser checks the CA's signature on it using the CA's public key from the certificate store. If the signature verifies, the name matches the site you asked for, and the certificate hasn't expired or been revoked, the browser trusts the bank's public key. If any check fails, you get a certificate warning.

Certificates sometimes have to be cancelled early, for example when a private key is stolen. Two ways to check: a certificate revocation list (CRL), a list of revoked serial numbers the CA publishes; and the Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP), which asks the CA about one certificate in real time.

PKI standards and certificate classes

Besides X.509, the Public-Key Cryptography Standards (PKCS) define formats for things like certificate requests and key storage. CAs also issue certificates at different classes, depending on how carefully they verify identity:

ClassUsed for
0Testing. No identity checks
1Individuals, with the focus on verifying email
2Organizations that need proof of identity
3Servers and software signing, with independent verification of identity and authority
4Online business transactions between companies
5Private organizations or government security

A higher class means more verification, and so more trust in the binding between name and key.

PKI topologies

Single-rootOne CA issues every certificate. Simple, but it's a single point of failure: if that CA is compromised, the whole PKI is.
HierarchicalA root CA certifies subordinate CAs, which issue the certificates. The root can stay offline and protected. Scales well. This is how the public internet works.
Cross-certifiedSeparate CAs certify each other, so users of one PKI can trust certificates from the other. Useful between partner organizations.

Using hashes to detect interception

A man-in-the-middle attacker can intercept a connection and present their own certificate. If it isn't signed by a trusted CA, the browser warns you. That's one reason never to click through a certificate warning. You can also check a certificate yourself: every certificate has a fingerprint, a hash of the whole certificate. Compare the fingerprint your browser shows with the real one, obtained through a separate trusted channel. If they differ, someone has substituted a certificate and is likely intercepting the traffic. SSH uses the same idea. The first time you connect to a server, it shows the server's key fingerprint for you to verify. If the fingerprint changes later, SSH warns loudly.

Concept 7 · Module 18.7

Applications and Impacts of Cryptography

Where PKI shows up every day, and what encryption does to a SOC's view of the network.

Common PKI applications

SSL/TLS and HTTPSCertificate-based authentication of web servers and encryption of web traffic
IPsec VPNsCertificates authenticate the VPN peers, securing traffic between sites or for remote users
802.1X network accessCertificates authenticate devices before they're allowed onto the wired or wireless network
S/MIME emailSigns and encrypts email messages
Code signingProves software and updates come from the real publisher, unaltered
Other usesSecure instant messaging, the Windows Encrypting File System (EFS), smart cards for two-factor authentication, and encrypted USB storage

Encryption's impact on security operations

Most web traffic is now encrypted with HTTPS, and that cuts both ways for defenders. Encryption protects users, but it also hides traffic from the tools that inspect it. An IPS or packet capture can see that a host is talking to a server on port 443, and for how long. It can't see what's inside. Attackers know this: malware increasingly uses HTTPS to download payloads and to talk to its command-and-control servers, because the traffic blends in.

Organizations respond in a few ways:

  • SSL/TLS decryption (SSL inspection): a device such as a decryption appliance or next-generation firewall decrypts traffic at the network edge, passes it to IPS and other inspection tools, then re-encrypts it. Users' devices must trust the appliance's certificate for this to work. Because it exposes private data, it needs clear policy, and categories like banking and health sites are often exempted.
  • Metadata analysis: even without decrypting, analysts can study who talks to whom, when, how much, and the details of the certificate and TLS handshake. An unusual certificate or a strange pattern can reveal malware without reading a byte of content.
  • Endpoint monitoring: traffic is plaintext on the endpoint before it's encrypted, so endpoint security tools can see what network tools can't.
For analysts: the course's point here is that a security analyst has to understand cryptography to do the job. You need to know what an alert can and can't see, recognize a certificate problem, and tell the difference between a protocol that's encrypted and one that isn't. Session 12 picks this up with how security technologies affect monitoring.

Choosing algorithms

Securing the depot link

Kaidō Freight is setting up a VPN between its headquarters and a new depot. A contractor proposes DES for the data and DH group 2 for the key exchange, "because they're supported everywhere." What would you recommend instead, and why does the VPN use two kinds of algorithm at all?

Show answer

Recommend: AES (128 or 256-bit) for the data, and DH group 14 or higher (or an elliptic curve group such as 19 or 20) for key exchange. DES's 56-bit key and DH group 2 are both too weak.

Why two: asymmetric DH lets the two sites agree on a shared key safely over the internet, but it's slow. Symmetric AES is fast enough to encrypt all the traffic, but needs a shared key first. Each covers the other's weakness.

Hashing passwords

The leaked customer database

Tsukimi Sweets' old online shop is breached, and the attacker posts the customer table. Passwords were stored as unsalted MD5 hashes. Within a day, most of them are cracked. Why was it so quick, and what should the new shop do differently?

Show answer

Why quick: unsalted hashes mean every customer with the same password has the same hash, and precomputed rainbow tables for MD5 already contain the hashes of common passwords. The attacker mostly looked them up. MD5 is also very fast to compute, so dictionary and brute-force guessing is fast too.

Do differently: add a unique random salt per user, and use a slow, purpose-built password hashing function (bcrypt, scrypt, or Argon2) instead of a general hash. Then force password resets for every customer, and warn them to change the password anywhere they reused it.

Obscuring data

Realistic test data

Kiriyama General Hospital's developers want a copy of the patient database to test a new scheduling app. Compliance says real patient data can't leave production. Encrypting the copy doesn't help, because the developers would decrypt it to use it. What technique fits?

Show answer

Data masking. Build the test copy by replacing names, dates of birth, and record numbers with realistic fake values (substitution) and mixing remaining fields between records (shuffling). The data keeps its shape, so the app can be tested properly, but no row describes a real patient.

Digital signatures

"I never placed that order"

A wholesale customer of Kaidō Freight claims they never approved a large shipping order and refuses to pay. The order was submitted through a portal that requires each customer to sign orders with a private key from a certificate Kaidō issued. Which property of digital signatures settles this, and what must be true for it to hold?

Show answer

Nonrepudiation. The order's signature verifies with the customer's public key, so it was produced with the customer's private key. A third party can accept that as proof the customer signed it.

What must be true: the private key stayed private. If the customer can show their key was stolen, and reported it so the certificate was revoked before the order, the argument weakens. That's why key protection and fast revocation (CRL or OCSP) matter.

PKI trust

The certificate warning at the clinic

Staff at a Kiriyama branch clinic report that the hospital's internal portal suddenly shows a certificate warning on the clinic's guest Wi-Fi, but not on the staff network. Several staff clicked "continue anyway." What might be happening, and how could an analyst confirm it?

Show answer

Possible cause: a man-in-the-middle on the guest network, such as a rogue access point, presenting its own certificate for the portal. Its certificate isn't signed by a CA the devices trust, so the browser warns.

Confirm: compare the certificate fingerprint (its hash) seen on guest Wi-Fi against the real one seen on the staff network. Different fingerprints mean a substituted certificate. Anyone who clicked through should change their portal password, and the guest network should be checked for rogue APs.

Impact on monitoring

Nothing in the IPS logs

Endpoint protection on a Tsukimi Sweets office PC flags malware, and the malware has clearly been talking to a server on the internet. The network IPS logged nothing suspicious from that PC. How is that possible, and what could Bastion use to see this traffic next time?

Show answer

How: the malware used HTTPS. The IPS saw an ordinary-looking encrypted connection to port 443 and couldn't inspect the content.

Options: SSL/TLS decryption at the edge so the IPS can inspect the traffic (with a policy covering privacy and exemptions); metadata analysis of destinations, timing, and certificate details; and relying on endpoint monitoring, which sees data before it's encrypted, as it did here.
